\begin{abstract}
Fusing radiomics and convolutional neural network (CNN) features is widely assumed to improve pulmonary nodule malignancy classification, but the size and reliability of that improvement over the stronger single modality is rarely tested with a matched, statistically powered protocol. We compare five representation arms -- CNN-only, radiomics-only, early, intermediate, and late fusion -- across seven CNN backbones on LIDC-IDRI, using patient-grouped five-fold cross-validation; only intermediate fusion's joint training uses a nested inner split for epoch selection, while the CNN checkpoint shared by the other arms is selected on the same fold it is later scored on (disclosed in full in Section~\ref{sec:integrity}). Radiomics-only reaches a pooled mean AUC of 0.9324; late (decision-level) fusion reaches 0.9349, and pairwise DeLong tests find no significant difference between the two at any of the seven backbones (largest deviation p equal to 0.204). Early and intermediate fusion, in contrast, are significantly worse than radiomics-only at seven and six of seven backbones respectively (p as low as 2.6e-7). A case-level analysis explains the ceiling: the CNN and radiomics branches agree on 87--89\% of held-out cases (Pearson correlation 0.79--0.83), and where they disagree, radiomics is uniquely correct 1.1 to 1.6 times as often as the CNN branch, at every backbone. We separately evaluate explainability per branch -- Grad-CAM/Layer-CAM pointing accuracy against radiologist-annotated masks for the CNN branch, SHAP feature attribution for the radiomics branch -- across twelve backbones, and find pointing accuracy (0.00--0.72) has no consistent relationship with classification AUC. We report this alongside a transparent reproducibility audit: fixing a nested-cross-validation epoch-selection leak cost the intermediate-fusion arm 0.021 AUC on its own, a correction larger than the entire fusion-vs-radiomics gap this paper reports, and an honest nested-checkpoint re-run on three backbones shows both that equivalence claim and the CNN branch's own pointing-accuracy numbers are checkpoint-protocol-dependent, a collapse an independent faithfulness measurement confirms is genuine rather than an artifact.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death worldwide, responsible for an estimated 1.8 million deaths in 2022 alone \cite{bray_globocan_2024}, and predicting whether a pulmonary nodule seen on computed tomography (CT) is malignant is one of the more consequential classification problems in medical imaging: both false negatives (a missed cancer) and false positives (an unnecessary biopsy) carry direct patient cost. Two families of methods dominate the recent literature: radiomics pipelines extract a large, hand-specified set of shape, intensity, and texture descriptors from a segmented nodule for a classical classifier, while deep-learning pipelines let a convolutional neural network (CNN) learn its own representation directly from the image patch. Because the two capture information differently -- statistically defined texture operators versus learned convolutional filters -- combining them is a natural and frequently proposed idea.

The published evidence for fusion is real but, on closer reading, more modest than headline numbers suggest. Zheng et al.\ report a multi-feature fusion model at 97.6\% accuracy on LIDC-IDRI against 97.0\% for their clinical/radiomic-only arm and 90.3\% for deep features alone \cite{zheng_multilevel_2026} -- a decisive win over the CNN, but under a single percentage point over the stronger single modality. Lin et al., fusing a 3D-CNN with radiomics and clinical variables on LUNA16, report 92.80\% accuracy for the fused model against 92.21\% for their CNN-only ablation and 90.75\% for radiomics-only \cite{lin_combined_2024}: again a clear win over the weaker modality, a marginal one over the stronger. Astaraki et al.'s controlled comparison on low-dose CT reports a best hybrid area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.938 against 0.921 for optimized radiomics alone and only 0.824 for end-to-end deep learning alone \cite{astaraki_comparative_2021} -- notable here because deep learning, not radiomics, is the \emph{weaker} modality once each is separately tuned. Prabhavalkar and Arthi's PET-CT fusion study is the outlier, with a large 80\%-to-95\% gain \cite{prabhavalkar_hybrid_2026} on a substantially different, metabolic-imaging task. None of these four studies report a significance test on the fusion-vs-best-single-modality gap specifically.

There is a concrete mechanistic reason to expect fusion gains to be small or absent rather than assume they generalize. Wu et al.\ show that multimodal networks trained end-to-end tend to greedily fit whichever modality is easiest to learn from, quantified as an asymmetric ``conditional utilization rate'' between modalities, and that this imbalance can leave a jointly trained network effectively ignoring the slower-learning modality unless training is explicitly rebalanced \cite{wu_greedy_2022}. Chang et al., fusing histopathology, radiology, and clinical data for prostate cancer recurrence, show the same failure mode from the other direction: fusing a strong modality (clinical, concordance index (C-index) 0.80) with a weak one (radiology, C-index 0.56) drove performance \emph{below} the strong modality alone (0.76 vs.\ 0.80), while fusing two comparably strong modalities produced a genuine synergistic gain \cite{chang_synergy_2025}. Read together, these two papers predict exactly the pattern this paper's ablation needs to check for: fusion helps only when both branches are independently competitive, and a naively trained joint network can underperform its stronger input on its own.

Beyond whether fusion helps at all, this paper also asks whether any resulting model can be trusted: explainability compounds the problem rather than resolving it. Even when a model is deployed successfully, a clinician has no principled way to trust a saliency map unless that map's fidelity has itself been validated -- and recent controlled studies suggest fidelity does not track accuracy. Tempel et al.\ show that Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping), computed from an attention layer, can perform at chance (AUC near 0.5) on a perturbation-faithfulness test even though the underlying classifier works correctly, and that SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) and Grad-CAM routinely disagree on which input regions matter \cite{tempel_choose_2025}. Qin benchmarks Grad-CAM, SHAP, and LIME on a near-ceiling-accuracy MRI classifier and finds explanation consistency still ranges from 0.33 to over 0.90 depending on method, despite near-identical model performance \cite{qin_consistency_2025}. If explanation reliability and classification accuracy are this decoupled elsewhere, a fusion study that reports only accuracy and only a single post-hoc saliency map on top is not actually answering the interpretability question a clinical reader would ask.

This paper asks both questions together -- is any fusion gain statistically real, and does classification competence predict trustworthy explanation -- and answers them with four contributions. First, a five-arm (CNN-only, radiomics-only, early, intermediate, late fusion) by seven-backbone by five-fold ablation with per-backbone DeLong significance testing; epoch selection for the intermediate-fusion arm runs under a nested inner split so it cannot leak from the evaluation fold, a protection the CNN checkpoint shared by the other four arms does not have (Section~\ref{sec:integrity}). Second, a case-level complementarity analysis that explains \emph{why} the fusion ceiling sits where it does, rather than stopping at the aggregate AUC table. Third, a transparent account of a nested-cross-validation leak found and corrected during this work, reported because its magnitude turned out to be large enough to matter for how the field should read fusion-gain numbers generally. Fourth, a per-branch explainability protocol -- Grad-CAM/Layer-CAM pointing accuracy against radiologist masks for the CNN branch, SHAP attribution for the radiomics branch, evaluated separately across twelve backbones because no validated metric yet unifies the two -- that tests whether classification competence predicts explanation quality on this task.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Radiomics--Deep-Learning Fusion for Lung Nodule Malignancy}\label{sec:related-fusion}
Beyond the studies discussed in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, MERA proposes a self-supervised, Vision-Transformer-based explanatory model that reaches diagnostic accuracy comparable to fully annotated baselines on LIDC using roughly 1\% of the annotation \cite{lu_mera_2026}; it is closer to a label-efficient, intrinsically explainable classifier than a radiomics-CNN fusion architecture, but its design of fusing multiple explanation \emph{views} (attention maps, latent-space retrieval, predicted radiologist attributes) rather than multiple feature \emph{sources} is a relevant alternative to post-hoc fusion of a single saliency method. Kılıç et al.'s DE-SAMNet fuses two CNN backbones (DenseNet121, EfficientNetB0) through a spatial attention module and reports accuracy above 99\% on a public lung-CT benchmark, using Grad-CAM purely for post-hoc visualization without a quantitative localization metric \cite{kilic_desamnet_2026}. Patro et al.'s NeuroExplain-Net combines Grad-CAM, SHAP, and Integrated Gradients for a four-class lung screening task, but the paper explicitly states it did not quantitatively validate whether the three explanations agree with each other or with expert annotation, and lists that as an open limitation \cite{patro_neuroexplain_2026} -- precisely the gap Sections~\ref{sec:xai-protocol} and~\ref{sec:xai-results} of this paper address with a measured, not asserted, pointing-accuracy protocol.

\subsection{Fusion Architecture and Modality-Weighting Mechanisms}\label{sec:related-mechanisms}
Beyond the theoretical results in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, Arevalo et al.\ propose the Gated Multimodal Unit (GMU), which learns a per-sample, per-feature gate deciding how much each modality contributes to a fused representation instead of fusing with a fixed rule, and show it outperforms naive concatenation and unweighted late-fusion averaging on multilabel movie-genre classification \cite{arevalo_gmu_2017}. This is directly relevant to the intermediate-fusion arm evaluated here, which fuses branches with a fixed concatenation-then-dense design by default (Section~\ref{sec:arms}); a GMU-gated variant was tested on three backbones, with results reported in Section~\ref{sec:variants}.

\subsection{Explainability Reliability and Label Ambiguity in LIDC-IDRI}\label{sec:related-xai}
Ambiguity in the LIDC-IDRI malignancy label itself is well documented independently of this paper's own experiments. Liao et al.\ split 2{,}568 rated nodules into consistent (394), inconsistent (1{,}048 -- the largest group, 41\% of the dataset), and low-reliability (766) subsets based on inter-radiologist agreement, and argue that collapsing an inconsistent rating like 1, 1, 5, 5 to the same mean label as a consistent 3, 3, 3, 3 discards real clinical disagreement \cite{liao_ambiguous_2022}. Konuksever et al.\ quantify this at a finer grain: of 1{,}880 multiply-rated nodules, 641 (34\%) have a normalized rating standard deviation above 0.4, versus 407 (22\%) rated consistently \cite{konuksever_dynamic_2026}. Wang et al.'s ExPN-Net is a relevant counterpoint on the fusion-vs-interpretability trade-off question: their ablation shows that adding a radiologist-characteristic prediction task (subtlety, calcification, margin, spiculation, etc.) alongside malignancy classification \emph{improved} the primary AUC rather than trading against it, evidence that characteristic-aware, explainability-oriented supervision is not inherently in tension with accuracy \cite{wang_expnnet_2024}.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Dataset and Cross-Validation Split}\label{sec:dataset}
The dataset combines LIDC-IDRI nodules with LUNA16-derived hard-negative candidates: 717 patients, 725 scans, slice thickness 0.45--5.0\,mm (median 1.25\,mm), and a median 3-D nodule diameter of 11.0\,mm (range 2.45--54.28\,mm). Each nodule's malignancy label is the median of the available radiologist ratings on a 1--5 scale; the binary classification arm used throughout this paper excludes nodules whose median rating is exactly 3 (indeterminate), leaving 937 benign and 454 malignant nodules (Table \ref{tab:classdist}). Data is split five ways at the patient level (not the nodule level) with a fixed seed, so no patient's nodules appear in more than one fold; per-fold binary-arm counts are given in Table \ref{tab:folddist} and sum exactly to the totals in Table \ref{tab:classdist}.

\subsection{Preprocessing}\label{sec:preprocessing}
Each nodule is represented as a 2.5-D patch: a stack of adjacent axial slices centered on the nodule, cropped to a fixed in-plane resolution. Hounsfield-unit intensities are clipped to $[-1000, 400]$ and linearly rescaled to $[0,1]$. All seven backbones are evaluated at a common input resolution of 96\,px -- chosen as the smallest multiple of 32 exceeding every backbone's minimum input requirement (Inception-v3, Xception, and Inception-ResNet-v2 all require at least 71--75\,px), so that no backbone is advantaged or disadvantaged relative to the others when its explainability output is later compared against theirs.

\subsection{Representation Arms}\label{sec:arms}
Five arms are compared (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). \emph{CNN-only} trains a backbone end-to-end with a softmax classification head. \emph{Radiomics-only} extracts PyRadiomics shape, first-order, and texture features from the segmented nodule, selects features per fold on the training split only, and classifies with XGBoost. \emph{Early fusion} concatenates the CNN's learned embedding with the radiomics feature vector and classifies the combined vector with XGBoost, so the CNN embedding is produced by a backbone already trained in its CNN-only configuration rather than trained jointly. \emph{Intermediate fusion} is a single end-to-end trainable network: the CNN branch is projected to a 256-dimensional vector and the radiomics branch to 128 dimensions, the two are concatenated (384-d) and passed through a joint dense head with a two-class softmax output, with 0.3 dropout on each branch projection. \emph{Late fusion} takes the independently trained CNN-only and radiomics-only models and averages their predicted probabilities, with no joint training step at all.

\subsection{Backbones}\label{sec:backbones}
Seven CNN backbones are evaluated: DenseNet121 \cite{huang_densenet_2017}, DenseNet201 \cite{huang_densenet_2017}, GoogLeNet \cite{szegedy_googlenet_2015}, InceptionV3 \cite{szegedy_inceptionv3_2016}, Xception \cite{chollet_xception_2017}, ConvNeXt-Tiny \cite{liu_convnext_2022}, and Inception-ResNet-v2 \cite{szegedy_inceptionresnet_2017}. DenseNet's dense connectivity pattern concatenates each layer's output with every subsequent layer, which the original authors show reduces parameter count for a given accuracy level and is reported to reduce overfitting on smaller training sets \cite{huang_densenet_2017} -- a relevant property given this dataset's size. GoogLeNet introduced the multi-branch Inception module with 1$\times$1 bottleneck convolutions to grow network capacity without a proportional compute increase \cite{szegedy_googlenet_2015}; Inception-v3 factorizes its larger convolutions into stacked smaller ones for further efficiency \cite{szegedy_inceptionv3_2016}; Xception replaces Inception's mixed convolutions with a stack of depthwise-separable convolutions, matching Inception-v3's parameter count at marginally higher ImageNet accuracy \cite{chollet_xception_2017}. ConvNeXt-Tiny modernizes a standard convolutional backbone with Vision-Transformer-inspired design choices -- large depthwise kernels, an inverted bottleneck, GELU activations, and LayerNorm -- while remaining fully convolutional, and its authors show it matches or exceeds comparably sized Vision Transformers on ImageNet without any attention mechanism \cite{liu_convnext_2022}. Inception-ResNet-v2 combines Inception's multi-branch convolutions with residual connections; the original authors report this combination accelerates training convergence relative to a similarly deep Inception network without residual connections, at comparable computational cost \cite{szegedy_inceptionresnet_2017}.

\subsection{Explainability Protocol}\label{sec:xai-protocol}
The CNN and radiomics branches are explained separately, since no validated metric currently puts a gradient-based saliency map and a SHAP feature-attribution vector on a common scale -- a gap NeuroExplain-Net's authors note explicitly as unresolved in their own work \cite{patro_neuroexplain_2026}. For the CNN branch, Grad-CAM and Layer-CAM activation maps are compared against radiologist-drawn nodule masks using Dice, Intersection-over-Union (IoU), size-matched Dice, pointing accuracy (whether the map's maximum-activation pixel falls inside the mask), and an energy-based pointing metric. Layer-CAM is used alongside Grad-CAM because Jiang et al.\ show Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM++ assign one global weight per channel, which is unreliable at shallow layers where per-location gradient variance is high, and that a spatially-varying, per-location weight (Layer-CAM's contribution) produces more reliable maps from earlier layers \cite{jiang_layercam_2021}; this class-activation-mapping family builds on earlier top-down attention propagation work such as Excitation Backprop, which also explicitly targets medical-imaging interpretability \cite{zhang_excitationbackprop_2018}. For the radiomics branch, SHAP values quantify each feature's contribution to the predicted probability. On the three backbones covered by the honest nested-checkpoint re-run (Section~\ref{sec:integrity}), we additionally test whether the CNN-branch map is faithful to the decision, not merely localized near it, with two mask-free perturbation families on the same fold-0-through-4 evaluation cases: ROAD, which imputes the most- and least-relevant pixels first (MoRF/LeRF) across nine percentiles \cite{rong_road_2022}, and the prediction gap on important versus unimportant pixels (PGI/PGU) \cite{agarwal_openxai_2022}.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Integrity Audit}\label{sec:integrity}
Five corrections materially affect the numbers reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} and are disclosed here rather than silently absorbed into the final figures. First, the original intermediate-fusion training loop selected its best checkpoint using AUC on the same outer validation fold it then reported -- an epoch-selection leak. Re-implementing this with a nested inner split (an 85/15 patient-grouped inner split drawn from the outer training fold, used only for checkpoint selection, with the outer validation fold touched exactly once after training) reduced intermediate fusion's pooled AUC by 0.021, while the other four arms moved by less than 0.0005 -- consistent with only the intermediate-fusion loop being exposed to the leak in the first place. This nested-split fix is scoped to intermediate fusion's own joint training only: the CNN checkpoint that CNN-only, early fusion, and late fusion all share (\texttt{stage\_03\_train.py}, reused by \texttt{stage\_03b\_fusion.py} for the fusion arms) is selected by validating on the same outer fold it is later scored on, across all seven backbones -- every CNN-only, early-fusion, and late-fusion number in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and Table~\ref{tab:delong} rests on that same-fold-selected (``Selected'') checkpoint, not a nested one. A supplementary check from a later, independent re-run (run02, three of the seven backbones) quantifies what that scope costs for late fusion specifically: comparing the Selected checkpoint against the unselected final-epoch (``Unselected'') checkpoint, the late-fusion-vs-radiomics-only DeLong test flips from a tie to a significant loss for fusion\_late at two of the three backbones tested (Table~\ref{tab:checkpointsensitivity}). Neither ``Selected'' nor ``Unselected'' here is a genuine nested-cross-validation checkpoint -- both are lower-bound sensitivity checks on the same non-nested procedure described above. A separate, later re-run under an honest nested checkpoint-selection regime (run03) found the fusion\_late-vs-radiomics-only equivalence does not hold at two of the same three backbones (ConvNeXt-Tiny $p=0.0013$; DenseNet201 $p=0.0003$; DenseNet121 still ties, $p=0.1558$) -- a materially larger and more direct threat to this paper's central equivalence claim than the sensitivity check above. That nested re-run is itself reported by its authors as a lower bound, not a final number, pending a cleaner single-stage nested retrain across all seven backbones that this paper does not undertake; we disclose the full scope of the non-nested CNN checkpoint procedure, and this open risk to it, rather than omit either. Second, radiomics feature selection via mutual information was found to vary by up to 0.0036 AUC across identical re-runs because scikit-learn's mutual-information estimator injects small random noise to break ties; fixing a seed made re-runs reproduce exactly. Third, three of DenseNet121's five fold checkpoints were found to have silently skipped retraining after an input-resolution change (a resume-from-checkpoint guard compared only epoch counts, not input size), leaving those three folds evaluated on weights trained at the old resolution; retraining those folds from scratch raised DenseNet121's standalone AUC from 0.8333 to 0.8940, in line with the other six backbones. All results in this paper use the retrained checkpoints. Fourth, the CNN-branch explainability metrics in Table~\ref{tab:xai} were computed once, before the DenseNet121 checkpoint fix above, and were not regenerated afterward; cross-checking against a later, independently re-run localization pass (same protocol, same fold-0 evaluation cases, run after the retrain) confirms ConvNeXt-Tiny and DenseNet201's original numbers reproduce within noise (dice within 0.001--0.012, pointing accuracy within 0.005--0.012), but DenseNet121's original pointing accuracy of 0.717 does not -- the re-run gives 0.576 (dice 0.063, size-matched dice 0.342, $n=59$ of 60 fold-0 cases, one excluded for a missing predicted probability). Table~\ref{tab:xai} reports the corrected DenseNet121 row; the original 0.717 value was carried by the same stale, pre-retrain checkpoint documented in the third correction above. These figures are reported here as the honest noise floor and correction history for interpreting the results below, not as caveats that were fixed and then hidden. Fifth, the same honest nested-checkpoint re-run (run03) that reopens the equivalence claim above also reopens the CNN-branch pointing-accuracy numbers in Table~\ref{tab:xai} for the three backbones it covers. Recomputing Layer-CAM from the nested checkpoints, at the same canonical explanation site and the same fold-0 evaluation cases, moves pointing accuracy from 0.717 to 0.500 (ConvNeXt-Tiny), from 0.717 to 0.250 (DenseNet121), and from 0.700 to 0.000 (DenseNet201); pooled over all five folds the same pattern holds (ConvNeXt-Tiny $0.500\pm0.081$, DenseNet121 $0.103\pm0.092$, DenseNet201 $0.000\pm0.000$ -- zero in every fold). The classifiers are unaffected: rescoring the loaded nested checkpoints reproduces their recorded outer AUC to the last digit on six of six cells (0.8178--0.8965), so this is not a silently mismatched \texttt{state\_dict}. A pre-registered artifact-exclusion protocol rules out a broken map: the canonical site never returns an all-zero activation map in any of the fifteen fold-backbone cells checked, while a shallower candidate site (\texttt{features.0.relu0}) recovers pointing accuracy of 0.350 (DenseNet201) and 0.417 (DenseNet121), well above the 0.019 chance rate estimated from the same sweep. What has moved under the nested protocol is the depth of the best explanation site, not merely its height -- the deepest site is best under the Selected checkpoint on all three backbones, and that peak shifts to the shallowest candidate site on both DenseNets under the nested one. The pointing-accuracy values in Table~\ref{tab:xai} are therefore reported as Selected-checkpoint numbers, not backbone-intrinsic ones, for these three backbones; the same caveat has not been checked for the other nine.

A non-significant DeLong $p$-value only fails to reject a difference; it does not positively establish that two arms perform the same. As a stronger, targeted check on the paper's central claim, we re-ran the fusion\_late-vs-radiomics-only comparison with the Two One-Sided Tests (TOST) procedure at a 0.02 AUC equivalence margin, fixed before the analysis on clinical grounds, for three of the seven backbones (ConvNeXt-Tiny, DenseNet201, DenseNet121) using the same nested-split checkpoint-selection regime as the rest of this paper. Fig.~\ref{fig:tost} shows all three 90\% confidence intervals fall entirely inside the margin -- fusion\_late is formally equivalent to radiomics-only at all three backbones tested, not merely statistically indistinguishable from it. As a validity check on the test itself, the same procedure applied to fusion\_late vs.\ cnn\_only (a comparison DeLong already finds significant in Table \ref{tab:delong}'s companion analysis) correctly rejects equivalence at all three backbones, confirming TOST is not simply underpowered to detect real gaps. This result covers three of seven backbones and is reported as a supplementary confirmatory check, not a replacement for Table \ref{tab:delong}.

\subsection{Modality Complementarity}\label{sec:complementarity}
Table \ref{tab:complement} reports, per fold and averaged over 273 held-out cases at a 0.5 decision threshold, how often the CNN branch and radiomics branch agree or disagree at the case level. Predicted probabilities from the two branches correlate strongly (Pearson $r=0.79$--0.83, Spearman $\rho=0.68$--0.74) across all seven backbones. Where the two branches disagree, the CNN branch is uniquely correct in roughly 14 cases per fold (about 5.1\%, stable across all seven backbones), while radiomics is uniquely correct in 16--22 cases (roughly 6--8\%) -- between 1.1 and 1.6 times as often as the CNN branch, at every backbone.

A separate question from localization is whether a map is faithful to the decision it explains. On the three backbones covered by the honest nested-checkpoint re-run, ROAD and PGI/PGU (Section~\ref{sec:xai-protocol}) agree with each other and with the pointing-accuracy collapse in Section~\ref{sec:integrity}: ConvNeXt-Tiny is faithful by both measures in five folds of five (MoRF $-0.1766$ vs.\ LeRF $-0.0151$, 257 of 300 nodules, PGI/PGU ratio 1.96); DenseNet121 is faithful in one fold of five ($-0.0527$ vs.\ $-0.1160$, 154 of 300, ratio 1.38); DenseNet201 is reversed in every fold ($-0.0709$ vs.\ $-0.1592$, 122 of 300, ratio 0.39) -- the pixels its map calls important move the decision \emph{less} than the pixels it calls unimportant. Three measurements sharing no perturbation machinery agreeing is what lets DenseNet201's map be read as uninformative about its own decision, not merely mislocalized. Because late fusion averages a fixed radiomics probability with the CNN probability and a pixel perturbation only touches the image, its ROAD score would equal exactly half the CNN-only branch's score by construction; late fusion's own faithfulness is therefore not reported separately here, since halving a signal (or halving its absence) is an arithmetic property of the averaging rule, not new evidence about the fused model.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The complementarity numbers in Table \ref{tab:complement} explain, rather than merely restate, the ceiling in Table \ref{tab:ablation}. A Pearson correlation of 0.79--0.83 between two branches' predicted probabilities means most of the classification signal is shared, not complementary; the residual 11--13\% disagreement is where any fusion gain must come from, and within that residual, radiomics wins about 1.1--1.6 times as often as the CNN branch, at every backbone. Averaging two branches under those conditions caps the achievable improvement at a small fraction of an AUC point, which is exactly the 0.0025 late-fusion margin observed -- not because the fusion mechanism is broken, but because there is arithmetically little complementary signal left for it to recover once the shared component is accounted for. This also situates the arm-specific pattern in Wu et al.'s and Chang et al.'s terms \cite{wu_greedy_2022,chang_synergy_2025}: intermediate fusion forces both branches through a single trained decision boundary, exactly the setting in which a faster-converging or higher-capacity branch can dominate the loss and suppress the weaker one; late fusion instead lets each branch reach its own independent optimum first and only averages two already-calibrated probability estimates afterward, which is structurally incapable of driving performance below the stronger branch's own probability the way a shared, jointly optimized feature space can. That distinction, not a difference in how much information the two feature sets nominally carry, is the more likely reason late fusion ties radiomics while early and intermediate fusion measurably lose to it.

The nested-cross-validation correction in Section~\ref{sec:integrity} is worth stating in the same breath as the headline result, because of its size relative to it. Fixing the epoch-selection leak cost intermediate fusion 0.021 AUC on its own -- roughly eight times the entire 0.0025 gap between late fusion and radiomics-only that this paper treats as its main finding. Put differently: a study using this same architecture and dataset, but without a nested inner split for checkpoint selection, could report an apparent fusion advantage nearly an order of magnitude larger than what nested validation actually supports, without any other change to the method. The supplementary Selected-vs-Unselected checkpoint comparison in Section~\ref{sec:integrity} sharpens this further: it is not only the size of a reported fusion gain that a nested split protects, but whether an equivalence claim is real at all -- two of three backbones checked flip from a statistical tie to a significant loss for fusion\_late once checkpoint selection reverts to an unselected final epoch (Table~\ref{tab:checkpointsensitivity}). This is offered as a quantitative caution, not an accusation against the fusion-positive results cited in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, none of which report enough methodological detail to determine whether an equivalent leak is present; it is a concrete number the field can use to judge how large a reported fusion gain needs to be before treating it as more likely signal than an artifact of this specific, easy-to-introduce leak. A more direct, still-unresolved test of this paper's central claim is also disclosed in Section~\ref{sec:integrity}: an honest nested checkpoint-selection re-run finds the equivalence gone at two of the three backbones checked, a risk this paper does not consider closed.

The explainability results in Table \ref{tab:xai} extend, with a concrete case, the pattern Tempel et al.\ and Qin report in unrelated domains: that saliency-map reliability does not track classification competence \cite{tempel_choose_2025,qin_consistency_2025}. GoogLeNet reaches a pooled DeLong-tested AUC on par with the other backbones (Table \ref{tab:delong} shows it losing no more or less often than its neighbors) while its Grad-CAM pointing accuracy is exactly zero -- the model is deciding correctly on evidence its own class-activation map does not localize inside the annotated nodule at all. ViT-Base shows the same zero-pointing pattern in Fig.~\ref{fig:xaicompare}, consistent with the class-activation-mapping formulation being tuned for convolutional feature hierarchies rather than transformer attention. ConvNeXt-Tiny and DenseNet201, at the opposite end (0.717 and 0.700), plausibly keep late-layer activations more spatially anchored to the original input region than architectures that aggressively pool or globally mix features earlier -- though this paper's data supports that as a plausible architectural correlate, not a proven causal mechanism, since only twelve backbones are compared and no controlled ablation isolates the pooling strategy as the variable responsible. DenseNet121 sits well below both at 0.576, which argues against feature-reuse depth alone being the explanation: DenseNet121 and DenseNet201 share the same dense-connectivity design and differ mainly in depth, yet land 0.124 apart. What the data do support directly is the practical conclusion: reporting only accuracy, or only a qualitative Grad-CAM image, would have missed this twelve-fold spread entirely, and a clinician shown a GoogLeNet or ViT-Base saliency map for this task would be looking at a heatmap with no more than chance overlap with where the actual nodule is. This ranking is itself conditioned on the Selected checkpoint used throughout Table~\ref{tab:xai}: recomputed under the honest nested-checkpoint re-run disclosed in Section~\ref{sec:integrity}, DenseNet201 falls from tied-highest to last of the three backbones checked, and the faithfulness measurement above shows the reversal is not confined to pointing accuracy -- DenseNet201's map is also the one most consistently unfaithful to its own decision under that same checkpoint. The architectural-correlate story two sentences above is therefore a property of one training protocol, and the honest-nested numbers argue against reading 0.717/0.700/0.576 as fixed backbone rankings at all.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

Most consequentially, the CNN checkpoint shared by CNN-only, early fusion, and late fusion across all seven backbones is selected by validating on the same fold it is later scored on, not a nested split (Section~\ref{sec:integrity}); a three-backbone honest-nested re-run found this paper's central late-fusion-vs-radiomics-only equivalence claim does not hold at two of the three backbones checked (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}), and the same re-run collapses CNN-branch pointing accuracy at all three backbones checked, most severely at DenseNet201 (0.700 to 0.000, zero in every fold), confirmed as genuine rather than an artifact by an independent faithfulness measurement (Section~\ref{sec:integrity}); both are open risks this paper discloses but has not resolved with a full seven-backbone nested retrain. A Gated Multimodal Unit \cite{arevalo_gmu_2017}, branch normalization, and modality dropout with an auxiliary loss were tested as intermediate-fusion variants, but only on three of the seven backbones (Section~\ref{sec:variants}), not the full ablation grid; all twelve variant-backbone combinations underperformed radiomics-only, consistent with the mechanistic argument in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} that intermediate fusion's shared decision boundary, not the specific gating or normalization mechanism, is the more likely bottleneck. Extending this follow-up to the remaining four backbones is unlikely to change that qualitative conclusion but is left as immediate future work for completeness. Grad-CAM/Layer-CAM and SHAP are reported on separate scales with no unified cross-modality metric, a gap this paper shares with, rather than resolves relative to, prior work in the same space \cite{patro_neuroexplain_2026}. The faithfulness measurement in Section~\ref{sec:xai-results} also carries a bias in its own favor: perturbation-based tests are reported to advantage spatial explanations over feature-attribution ones \cite{tempel_choose_2025}, and this paper compares a spatial CAM channel against a feature-level SHAP channel, so ROAD and PGI/PGU rank backbones within the spatial channel and are not read here as ranking the two explanation channels against each other. Radiomics feature selection in this pipeline falls back to mutual-information selection rather than the originally intended minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance (mRMR) method whenever the \texttt{pymrmr} dependency is unavailable; this fallback was verified to be seed-stable and is not believed to change the substance of the radiomics-only results, but it is a methods correction disclosed here for completeness rather than a limitation discovered by an external reviewer.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Across seven CNN backbones and 21 pairwise significance tests, decision-level fusion of radiomics and CNN branches statistically ties the stronger single modality on this dataset, while feature-level (early) and jointly trained (intermediate) fusion measurably underperform it. The mechanism is visible in the data: the two branches agree on the large majority of cases, and where they disagree, the modality this paper's fusion arms are being asked to add value \emph{beyond} -- radiomics -- is already winning the disagreement more often than the CNN branch is. A field that wants to report fusion gains reliably should test each fusion arm against its strongest single-modality baseline with a significance test, use a nested split for any checkpoint or hyperparameter selection so that the resulting gap is not partly a leak, and validate any accompanying saliency map's localization quality rather than presenting it only as an illustration -- because, as this paper's own explainability results show, a model can classify competitively while its saliency map points nowhere near the lesion at all.
