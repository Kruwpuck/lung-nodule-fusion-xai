\begin{abstract}
Fusing radiomics with convolutional neural network (CNN) features is expected to improve pulmonary nodule malignancy classification, but the studies we reviewed do not test the gain over the stronger single modality. On the Lung Image Database Consortium and Image Database Resource Initiative (LIDC-IDRI) collection, we compare five arms (CNN-only, radiomics-only, early, intermediate, and late fusion) across seven CNN backbones with patient-grouped five-fold cross-validation. Radiomics-only reaches a mean area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.9324 and late fusion 0.9349; no DeLong test between them is significant at any backbone ($p\geq0.204$; each against its own radiomics refit). Early and intermediate fusion are worse: nine of 21 fusion-versus-radiomics tests remain significant after Holm correction, all against fusion. At a 0.5 threshold the branches agree on 87--89\% of cases, and where they disagree radiomics is right 1.1 to 1.6 times as often as the CNN. A single size feature from the reader outline, used directly as a score, reaches an AUC of 0.912, 0.021 below radiomics-only and above CNN-only (0.902; not tested). Against one fixed radiomics model, late fusion is equivalent within $\pm0.02$ AUC with the best or the final epoch on the evaluation fold, but significantly worse at two of three backbones under a nested recipe (unadjusted $p=0.0044$ and $0.0009$). Choosing the epoch of intermediate fusion on an inner split rather than on the evaluation fold lowers its AUC by 0.02 over six backbones. In this setting, no fusion strategy exceeds radiomics alone in AUC.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Lung cancer caused about 1.8 million deaths in 2022 \cite{bray_globocan_2024}, and deciding whether a pulmonary nodule on computed tomography (CT) is malignant drives the choice between surveillance and biopsy. Computer-aided decision support for this task follows two families. Radiomics extracts hand-specified shape, intensity, and texture descriptors from a segmented nodule for a classical classifier, while CNNs learn a representation from the image patch. Because the two capture information differently, combining them is a natural design choice for an intelligent decision-support system, and a fused model is only worth its extra complexity if it beats the better single model.

The published gains are small relative to that bar. On LIDC-IDRI, Zheng et al.\ report that multi-feature fusion exceeds the stronger of clinical radiomic features and deep features by 0.6 accuracy points \cite{zheng_multilevel_2026}. On LUNA16, Lin et al.\ report 0.59 points over their deep-learning ablation \cite{lin_combined_2024}, and Astaraki et al.\ report 0.002 AUC over their strongest single model, deep-feature radiomics \cite{astaraki_comparative_2021}. In each study the margin over the stronger single model is at most 0.6 accuracy points or 0.002 AUC, and Zheng et al.\ and Lin et al.\ report no significance test for that margin.

A mechanism predicts such small gains. Jointly trained multimodal networks tend to fit the modality that is fastest to learn and under-use the other \cite{wu_greedy_2022}, and fusing a strong with a weak modality can fall below the strong one alone (concordance index 0.76 against 0.80, in a 95-patient prostate cancer cohort), whereas fusing two comparably strong modalities helps \cite{chang_synergy_2025}. Fusion should therefore help only when both branches are independently competitive.

We ask whether any fusion strategy exceeds the stronger single modality, radiomics, and whether that answer depends on how the CNN checkpoint is chosen. Our contributions are: (i) a per-backbone test of each fusion strategy against the stronger single modality, with multiplicity control; (ii) an audit showing that late-fusion equivalence with radiomics does not survive a nested training recipe and that selecting the epoch on the evaluation fold inflates intermediate fusion; and (iii) a case-level analysis of branch agreement and size-only baselines that explain the ceiling. A companion study [BLINDED: companion paper reference] analyzes CNN class-activation maps on the same cohort.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

Whether fusion helps depends first on which single modality is stronger, and the literature does not agree on that. On 1007 LIDC-IDRI nodules cropped to 64 pixels, Saied et al.\ find a simple CNN ahead of texture radiomics by 0.075 AUC \cite{saied_efficient_2023}, whereas Astaraki et al.\ find tuned radiomics ahead of end-to-end deep learning by 0.097 \cite{astaraki_comparative_2021}. Ma et al.\ raise LIDC-IDRI accuracy from 91.66\% for five fused CNNs to 92.03\% by adding radiomics, with no radiomics-only arm and no significance test \cite{ma_novel_2023}. These studies differ in cohort, crop, and protocol, so their absolute values cannot be compared with one another or with ours; what they show is that a fusion study has to establish its own stronger branch before claiming a gain.

Fusion designs fall into early, intermediate, and late fusion, as a review of 69 healthcare fusion studies from 2018 to 2024 groups them \cite{teoh_fusion_2024}. Learned weighting of modalities, such as gated units, is one intermediate-fusion design, and whether it helps on small medical cohorts, where one branch is already strong, is an empirical question that the variants in Section~\ref{sec:ablation} address. The broader evidence also argues for caution: re-implementing 19 multimodal methods on nine datasets under standardized tuning and testing, Rheude et al.\ found that complex architectures do not reliably beat unimodal or simple baselines \cite{rheude_fusion_2025}.

A fusion model is only as credible as its evaluation, and information from the evaluation data that reaches model fitting or selection inflates reported performance; a survey of machine-learning-based science found such leakage in 294 papers across 17 fields \cite{kapoor_leakage_2023}. Choosing a CNN checkpoint epoch on the evaluated fold is, in our reading, one form of this leakage, and selecting the early-stopping point during cross-validation yielded ROC-AUC above 90\% on random data in simulations \cite{rajabi_early_2024}. Radiomics adds a second concern, because its features depend on segmentation and non-reproducible features should be excluded \cite{neha_radiomics_2026}. Finally, LIDC-IDRI labels are ambiguous: only 394 of 2568 rated nodules are rated consistently \cite{liao_ambiguous_2022}, and 641 of 1880 multiply rated nodules have a rating standard deviation above 0.4 \cite{konuksever_dynamic_2026}, which bounds the AUC any modality can reach on binarized labels.

These strands leave a gap in the fusion literature on LIDC-IDRI. Zheng et al., Lin et al., and Ma et al.\ report no significance test for the margin of the fused model over its stronger single modality, and we found in the main texts of these papers no description of how the evaluated checkpoint was chosen and no size-only reference score. The experiments that follow address these three points.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Data and Split}\label{sec:dataset}
We use LIDC-IDRI \cite{armato_lidcidri_2011}, obtained from The Cancer Imaging Archive \cite{clark_tcia_2013}. A nodule is a cluster of reader annotations with a non-empty 50\% reader-consensus mask; nodules marked by a single reader are kept. Each label is the median malignancy rating (1--5); benign is a median below 3 and malignant above 3. This yields 2138 nodules from 717 patients (725 scans); the 747 with median 3 are excluded, leaving 1391 nodules (937 benign, 454 malignant) from 611 patients (619 scans). The radiomics table has no scan identifier, so 25 of these nodules whose patient and nodule index are ambiguous are excluded, leaving 1366 (928 benign, 438 malignant; 607 patients); all arms are scored on this set. Folds are assigned by patient, five-fold and stratified on each patient's modal grade (seed 42), so no patient appears in two folds (Table~\ref{tab:cohort}).

\subsection{Arms and Backbones}\label{sec:arms}
Each nodule is a 2.5-D patch: the three central axial slices of a $64\times64$ crop (1 mm isotropic voxels, 64 mm field of view) centered on the nodule, with Hounsfield units clipped to $[-1000,400]$ and rescaled to $[0,1]$, resized to 96 px inside the model. Seven ImageNet-pretrained backbones are used: DenseNet121, DenseNet201, GoogLeNet, InceptionV3, Xception, and Inception-ResNet-v2, from the architecture families reviewed for lung CT in \cite{abdullahi_review_2025}, and ConvNeXt-Tiny \cite{liu_convnext_2022}.

\emph{CNN-only} trains a backbone end-to-end with AdamW, whose convergence and generalization are analyzed in \cite{zhou_adamw_2024} (learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, cosine schedule), batch size 16, up to 50 epochs with early stopping on validation AUC (patience 10), and random flips and rotations up to $\pm15^\circ$. \emph{Radiomics-only} extracts shape, first-order, and texture features with PyRadiomics 3.1.0 inside the consensus mask (a library also used in a recent LIDC-IDRI fusion study \cite{zheng_multilevel_2026}), selects 14 to 27 of 1130 candidate features per fold on the training folds by mutual information (minimum-redundancy maximum-relevance, mRMR, was unavailable), and classifies with XGBoost 2.0.3 at library-default parameters. \emph{Early fusion} concatenates the trained CNN-only embedding with the radiomics vector for XGBoost. \emph{Intermediate fusion} projects the CNN branch to 256 and the radiomics branch to 128 dimensions, concatenates them, and trains a joint dense head end-to-end (dropout 0.3 per projection). \emph{Late fusion} averages the probabilities of the CNN-only and radiomics-only models with equal weights.

As trivial references, \emph{size-only baselines} score each nodule by the maximum 3-D diameter or the mesh volume of its consensus mask, used directly as the score or passed to XGBoost, and by two or all 14 shape features with XGBoost. For direct scores the feature value is the score, with larger values assumed more malignant a priori; no orientation was chosen after seeing an AUC. Three intermediate-fusion variants (a gated multimodal unit, branch normalization, and modality dropout with an auxiliary loss) were run on three backbones.

\subsection{Checkpoint Selection and Statistics}\label{sec:integrity}
Three CNN checkpoint regimes are compared. \emph{Selected}: the epoch with the best AUC on the outer fold that is then scored, used for CNN-only, early, and late fusion in the main experiment; this is not nested. \emph{Final-epoch}: the last epoch trained, where early stopping (patience 10) or the 50-epoch limit ended the run. \emph{Nested}: two-stage fine-tuning with the epoch chosen on an inner split of the training folds, run for ConvNeXt-Tiny, DenseNet201, and DenseNet121; it differs from the main recipe in training as well as in where the epoch is chosen, so its CNN branch is a lower bound and the effect of selection alone is not isolated. Intermediate fusion in the main experiment selects its epoch on an 85/15 patient-grouped inner split. The same arm was also run under the protocol that chose that epoch on the evaluated fold; the two protocols are compared in Section~\ref{sec:regimes}, and the CNN-only AUC is identical to four decimals under both at six of the seven backbones.

Statistical reporting follows the Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging (CLAIM) \cite{tejani_claim_2024}. Each fusion arm is compared with radiomics-only at each backbone by DeLong's test, as used for radiomics and deep-learning comparisons in \cite{astaraki_comparative_2021}, on predictions pooled over the five folds (21 tests, one family), with Holm correction across the 21. Tables report the mean of the five fold AUCs and the sample standard deviation (SD) over folds, or over the 35 backbone-fold cells for arm means; tests use pooled predictions, and the two estimators can differ by up to 0.02 AUC for one model.

``Tie'' means no significant DeLong difference. ``Equivalent'' means that two one-sided tests (TOST) reject a difference beyond $\pm0.02$ AUC, using the 90\% DeLong interval; the margin is a judgment of the smallest difference of practical interest rather than a derived clinical threshold, and as a control late fusion versus CNN-only must fail equivalence. Because nodules from one patient are not independent, the late-versus-radiomics difference is also bootstrapped over patients (10000 resamples, percentile intervals). A seeded refit of radiomics-only (seed 42 for the mutual-information step and XGBoost) gives one radiomics model for all backbones, the \emph{seed-fixed} model. Table~\ref{tab:regimes} recomputes late fusion as the mean of the stored CNN probability and the seed-fixed radiomics probability and applies Holm correction to its nine tests.

The two branches \emph{agree} on a nodule when they predict the same class at a 0.5 threshold, that is, both correct or both wrong; Pearson and Spearman correlations of the predicted probabilities are computed per fold and averaged, and the Youden threshold of each branch is chosen on the other four folds. Holm correction, the patient bootstrap, the seed-fixed comparator, Tables~\ref{tab:regimes} and~\ref{tab:selection}, the size-only baselines, and the two figures were added post hoc.

\subsection{Implementation}\label{sec:impl}
CNN training uses seed 42 (Python, NumPy, PyTorch) and deterministic cuDNN, so runs are not guaranteed bit-exact; the intermediate-fusion head and its shuffling are not seeded. Software: Python 3.11.9, PyTorch 2.3.0 (CUDA 12.1), XGBoost 2.0.3, PyRadiomics 3.1.0. Hardware: one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 (12 GB), Windows 10.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Fusion Ablation}\label{sec:ablation}
No fusion arm is better than radiomics-only at any backbone (Table~\ref{tab:delong}, Fig.~\ref{fig:arms}). Table~\ref{tab:delong} gives DeLong $p$ on pooled predictions with Holm-adjusted $p$ over the 21 tests in parentheses; the CNN-based arms use the Selected checkpoint, and intermediate fusion selects its epoch on an inner split. Averaged over seven backbones and five folds, radiomics-only reaches $0.9324\pm0.0147$ and CNN-only $0.9018\pm0.0211$ (mean $\pm$ SD over 35 cells). Late fusion is 0.0025 above radiomics-only and does not differ from it at any backbone ($p\geq0.204$). Uncorrected, early fusion is worse at all seven backbones and intermediate fusion at six; after Holm correction nine tests remain significant, five for early and four for intermediate fusion, with the weakest at $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.044$. The three intermediate-fusion variants (gated multimodal unit, branch normalization, modality dropout with an auxiliary loss), run on ConvNeXt-Tiny, DenseNet121, and DenseNet201, all score below the same backbone's radiomics-only AUC, by 0.005 to 0.024 across the twelve variant-backbone combinations, and no variant is best on all three backbones.

At a 0.5 threshold (Table~\ref{tab:metrics}, Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion}; Table~\ref{tab:metrics} gives means over 35 backbone-fold cells on 1366 nodules, and the figure sums counts over folds and averages over backbones) late fusion is the best on accuracy (0.887 against 0.876 for radiomics-only), specificity (0.940 against 0.925), precision, F1, and the threshold-free Brier score (lower is better; 0.088 against 0.094), with sensitivity equal to radiomics-only (0.773 in both; difference 0.0003), so its gain comes from fewer false positives, not from finding more malignant nodules. CNN-only is the lowest on accuracy (0.858), sensitivity (0.727), and F1 (0.766). These differences are untested and coexist with a non-significant DeLong difference between late fusion and radiomics-only; 0.5 is a default, not a calibrated operating point, malignant nodules are 32\% of the cohort (438 of 1366), the radiomics-only row is each backbone's own refit and carries its feature-selection noise, and the Selected checkpoint favors the arms that use the CNN.

Radiomics does not depend on the backbone, but each backbone's run refit it with an unseeded mutual-information step, so its AUC scatters from 0.9301 to 0.9343 (range 0.0042). The seed-fixed refit gives one model for all backbones, with mean fold AUC $0.9326\pm0.0174$ (pooled 0.9320), inside that scatter. The scatter exceeds the 0.0025 late-versus-radiomics margin, so that margin cannot be distinguished from seed noise; it is much smaller than the Nested losses (0.013 to 0.015) and the 0.02 equivalence margin. Table~\ref{tab:delong} still compares each backbone with its own refit (see Limitations), whereas Table~\ref{tab:regimes} uses the seed-fixed model.

Under Selected and Final-epoch checkpoints late fusion is equivalent to radiomics-only at all three backbones, and, as a control, equivalence with CNN-only is rejected in all six cases. Best versus final epoch within one recipe therefore does not change the verdict; the loss appears only under the Nested recipe, which changes both where the epoch is chosen and how the CNN is trained, so the two cannot be separated here; the Nested CNN branch is a lower bound for that recipe (Fig.~\ref{fig:regimes} shows the pooled differences with patient-level 90\% intervals over 607 patients and 10000 resamples). Under Nested checkpoints late fusion is significantly worse at ConvNeXt-Tiny ($p=0.0044$; $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.035$ over the nine tests of Table~\ref{tab:regimes}) and at DenseNet201 ($p=0.0009$; $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0078$), and equivalence cannot be established at either: the lower bound of the nodule-level 90\% interval is $-0.0208$ and $-0.0231$, the first only 0.0008 beyond the margin. \subsection{Modality Complementarity}\label{sec:complementarity}
The two branches mostly agree. Across the seven backbones, their predicted probabilities correlate strongly (mean over folds: Pearson $r=0.79$--0.83, Spearman $\rho=0.68$--0.74), and at a 0.5 threshold they agree on 87--89\% of the $\approx273$ cases per fold. Where they disagree, the CNN alone is correct in 13 to 16 cases per fold (5--6\%) and radiomics alone in 16 to 22 (6--8\%), 1.1 to 1.6 times as often (Selected checkpoint). With Youden thresholds chosen on the other four folds, checked on three backbones, radiomics still wins 1.3 to 1.9 times as often.

\subsection{Size-Only Baselines}\label{sec:size}
Nodule size alone is a strong reference (Table~\ref{tab:size} gives the mean and SD of the five fold AUCs and the AUC of pooled predictions; ``Direct'' rows use the feature value as the score, with no fitted model). The consensus-mask mesh volume used directly as a score reaches a mean fold AUC of 0.912, and the maximum 3-D diameter 0.903, on the same 1366 nodules; voxel volume differs from mesh volume by 0.0001. Both match or exceed CNN-only (0.9018; differences not tested), and on average CNN-only, early fusion, and intermediate fusion do not exceed mesh volume alone (Fig.~\ref{fig:arms}), and volume lies 0.021 below radiomics-only (0.9326) and 0.023 below late fusion (0.9349). Fitting XGBoost to the same features is lower (0.879 for diameter, 0.889 for volume), and 14 shape features reach 0.900. The room left for texture and intensity features, and for the CNN, is therefore about 0.02 AUC.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The null result is easiest to explain by how much the two branches share. With branch correlations near 0.8, most classification signal is shared, and the 11--13\% of cases where the branches disagree are the only place a gain can arise. Radiomics already wins most of them, so averaging can add little; the observed margin is 0.0025. Intermediate fusion, which trains both branches jointly, lost to the stronger branch, as greedy-learning and strong-plus-weak results predict \cite{wu_greedy_2022,chang_synergy_2025}. Early fusion, which feeds frozen CNN embeddings to XGBoost, also lost, and averaging two independently trained models came closest. Averaging does not guarantee the stronger branch's performance, however; under Nested checkpoints it lost at two backbones.

How the checkpoint is chosen matters at least as much as how the branches are fused. Choosing the epoch of intermediate fusion on the evaluated fold inflated its AUC by about 0.02 on average at six backbones (Table~\ref{tab:selection}), more than the late-versus-radiomics differences (0.005 to 0.015) that separate equivalence from a significant loss in Table~\ref{tab:regimes}. For late fusion itself, best versus final epoch did not change the verdict, so the loss under Nested checkpoints is not attributable to selection alone. Because selection on the evaluated fold favors the arms that take the CNN checkpoint (CNN-only, early, and late fusion), the null result against radiomics is conservative toward those arms. A fusion advantage of a few thousandths of AUC, as reported in the literature above, is smaller than the selection effect measured here, so it should be read alongside how checkpoints and hyperparameters were selected.

A further reason the comparison favors radiomics is what each branch is given. Radiomics features are computed inside the readers' consensus outline. Because the same readers outlined the nodules and rated their malignancy, outline-based shape features may partly encode the ratings, and the size-only baselines show how much: volume from the outline alone reaches 0.912, 0.010 above the CNN-only arm (difference not tested). The CNN branch has therefore not been shown to extract more than size from the patch, and the 0.021 gap between radiomics and volume is the room that remains for any other feature. A decision-support system that uses radiomics would need an automatic segmentation at inference, and the radiomics advantage measured here may shrink with it.

These findings bear on whether fusion is worth deploying. Fusion adds a second model, a second preprocessing chain, and a segmentation dependency. On this cohort that cost bought no measurable AUC gain, although late fusion's accuracy at a fixed 0.5 threshold was higher (untested), and the parity of late fusion with radiomics did not survive a nested recipe. A deployment decision should therefore rest on the stronger single model unless a fusion gain survives nested selection and a patient-level test.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
The main experiment uses the non-nested Selected checkpoint for three of five arms; the Nested re-run covers three backbones with a different recipe and is a lower bound, not a final estimate. The comparison of the two epoch-selection protocols for intermediate fusion is between two separate runs, and part of it may be feature-selection noise. Likewise, Table~\ref{tab:delong} compares each backbone with its own radiomics refit (0.9301--0.9343, seed noise); recomputing it against the seed-fixed model would require retraining intermediate fusion and early fusion and was not done. No arm was tuned separately: XGBoost used library defaults and every CNN used one recipe, and early fusion trains XGBoost on embeddings of training-fold nodules from a CNN trained on those nodules, which handicaps that arm.

Case-level counts at a fixed threshold were checked against Youden thresholds on three backbones only. The patient-level bootstrap also covers three backbones, and the 21 DeLong tests treat nodules as independent, so the count of nine significant tests would shrink if the standard errors were wider, although no fusion arm exceeds radiomics in any case. The $\pm0.02$ equivalence margin is a judgment. Operating-point metrics are reported at one untuned 0.5 threshold without tests, and calibration is assessed only by the Brier score.

Excluding median-3 and keeping single-reader nodules defines a cohort that differs from prior studies, so absolute AUCs are not directly comparable, and all results come from one dataset. CNN-only reaches 0.902 on a 64 px crop, below a simple CNN in another LIDC-IDRI study (0.960 \cite{saied_efficient_2023}), so the CNN branch may be weaker than a tuned model and the conclusions concern this setting. The CNN sees three slices of a 64 mm crop rather than the full 3-D volume, which may understate it. Radiomics and the size-only baselines use the reader-drawn consensus mask, which the CNN does not see, so they share its information advantage; no intensity-only baseline was run.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Across seven backbones, no radiomics--CNN fusion strategy exceeded radiomics alone in AUC on LIDC-IDRI. Decision-level fusion was equivalent to radiomics when the CNN epoch was the best or the final epoch on the evaluation fold, and was significantly worse at two of three backbones under a nested recipe with the epoch chosen on an inner split; early and intermediate fusion were significantly worse at nine of 21 comparisons (Holm-adjusted $p<0.05$). The branches agree on most cases, and radiomics sees the readers' outlines: nodule volume from the outline alone reaches an AUC of 0.912, 0.021 below radiomics and above the CNN-only arm. Fusion claims should be tested against the stronger single model. They should also use checkpoints chosen on an inner split, a patient-level unit of analysis, and a segmentation-aware baseline.
