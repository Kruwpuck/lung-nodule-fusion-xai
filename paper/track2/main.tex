\begin{abstract}
Explainability comparisons across deep learning backbones often report one class activation map (CAM) score per network, at a layer chosen by a heuristic. We test what such a score measures. For lung nodule malignancy classification on the Lung Image Database Consortium and Image Database Resource Initiative (LIDC-IDRI) collection, we sweep 86 candidate explanation sites across twelve backbones with Layer-CAM and score each by pointing accuracy against reader-consensus masks on 60 validation nodules. Because every crop is centered on its nodule, a parameter-free center map scores 0.983, above every Layer-CAM score at every site (maximum 0.733), so these scores do not show that a model localizes the lesion. The score still depends strongly on the site: within the candidate set the canonical last-spatial-layer site is the best in only four backbones, and in six the best site beats it by 0.18 to 0.63 (paired McNemar test, Holm-adjusted $p<0.005$, descriptive). Scores are consistent with a geometric effect: on feature maps of at most six cells per side, odd-sized maps average 0.498 over seven sites from five backbones, and even-sized maps 0.044. Automatic site selection can also fail silently: 12 sites emit an identically-zero map yet return a finite Dice of 0.035, and our band rule resolved GoogLeNet to such a site, scoring it 0.000. After retraining three backbones with a different protocol, the canonical score falls for all three. CAM scores should be reported with the site, its spatial size, its zero-map count, and, for centered crops, a center-map baseline.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A decision-support system for lung nodule assessment is expected to show a clinician where it looks, and class activation maps (CAMs) are among the most common ways to do so \cite{bhati_xai_2024}. Some studies that compare backbones report a CAM score next to accuracy: one map is computed per network, overlapped with a lesion mask, and the networks are ranked. That ranking fixes a second variable, the layer at which the map is taken, which we call the explanation site. Tooling often resolves this layer automatically, for example as the final convolutional layer or by a size heuristic, and the choice is not always reported. If the score depends on that choice, two studies can rank the same networks differently, and a clinician cannot tell whether a poorly localized map reflects the model or the tool that produced it.

We ask what such a score measures and how much it depends on that explanation site. We sweep every resolution stage of twelve backbones trained for malignancy classification on LIDC-IDRI \cite{armato_lidcidri_2011}, holding weights, samples, and metric code fixed, so that each per-backbone curve is its own control, and we compare every CAM with model-free maps.

Our contributions are three. (i) A model-free center map is a baseline that outscores every Layer-CAM score at every site, and a grid-parity tabulation shows how the map grid interacts with a centered lesion; together they bound what pointing accuracy on centered crops can show. (ii) A per-backbone sweep over 86 candidate sites, with paired tests and a split-half check, quantifies the dependence on the site, and a retraining check on three backbones shows that the canonical score changes with the training protocol. (iii) A census of silent failures in automatic site selection identifies the mechanism that keeps them silent.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

The explanation site matters because of how a CAM is built. A CAM weights the feature maps of one layer by class evidence, conventionally the final convolutional layer \cite{bhati_xai_2024}, and that choice is not neutral. Grad-CAM assumes that spatial organization survives the forward pass; for models with large receptive fields it does not, and the same study describes empty maps, feature-map rectification, and the influence of global average pooling or flatten layers on explanation quality \cite{santos_caveats_2025}. CAM-based methods can also assign importance to regions the network cannot see, which has been shown theoretically for Grad-CAM on a masked convolutional neural network (CNN) at initialization and experimentally on a VGG-like model trained to ignore part of the image \cite{taimeskhanov_walls_2024}. Most CAM-based methods share this behavior; HiResCAM, which is built to highlight only locations the model used, is the exception \cite{draelos_hirescam_2021}. Layer-CAM, the method swept here, and other variants have been compared on mammography \cite{sampaio_mammography_2023}.

Saliency evaluations in medical imaging show what such maps can and cannot do. On pneumothorax and pneumonia radiographs, all eight common saliency methods failed at least one of the localization, randomization-sensitivity, repeatability, or reproducibility criteria and were inferior to localization networks (pneumothorax area under the precision-recall curve, AUPRC, 0.024 to 0.224 against 0.404 for a U-Net); five of the eight failed the model-randomization test on the segmentation data \cite{arun_trustworthiness_2021}. In a larger chest radiograph benchmark, seven methods all localized pathologies worse than a human benchmark, with the largest gap for small and complex-shaped findings \cite{saporta_benchmarking_2022}, the regime of small pulmonary nodules. The effectiveness of Grad-CAM on pneumothorax radiographs varies across CNN and vision transformer (ViT) architectures \cite{qiu_gradcam_2023}. On lung cancer computed tomography (CT), an arXiv preprint accepted at the 18th International Conference on Knowledge and Smart Technology combines localization accuracy, perturbation-based faithfulness, and explanation consistency across five architectures and reports that Grad-CAM fidelity degrades for a ViT relative to the four CNNs \cite{panboonyuen_seeing_2026}. The studies whose layer choice we could check use one site per network. Other recent lung-CT studies mostly use Grad-CAM qualitatively: DE-SAMNet presents visualizations without a localization metric \cite{kilic_desamnet_2026}, and NeuroExplain-Net reports that its explanations were not validated against annotated lesions \cite{patro_neuroexplain_2026}.

Those evaluations differ in the metrics and baselines they use. Quantus groups 27 explanation-evaluation metrics into six categories, six of them for localization \cite{hedstrom_quantus_2023}. Localization is distinct from faithfulness, which ROAD evaluates by removing pixels \cite{rong_road_2022}. Lung-CT studies that score localization use pointing accuracy, as in Katar et al.\ against radiologist boxes at the final attention block \cite{katar_transformer_2026}, or overlap with the tumor mask \cite{panboonyuen_seeing_2026}. On chest radiographs, Arun et al.\ score a model-free baseline, a single average mask of all ground-truth segmentations or boxes \cite{arun_trustworthiness_2021}. None of the lung-CT studies we reviewed reports a model-free baseline, although crops centered on the finding make one easy to construct.

Taken together, prior work shows that saliency maps can be unreliable in medical imaging and that CAM methods have failure modes. To our reading, it does not (i) vary the explanation layer within each network and test the differences, (ii) compare localization scores with a model-free baseline on centered crops, or (iii) report how often automatic layer selection returns a constant map. We address these three points in one lung-nodule cohort.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Data, Labels, and Split}\label{sec:data}
LIDC-IDRI \cite{armato_lidcidri_2011} was obtained from The Cancer Imaging Archive \cite{clark_tcia_2013}. A nodule is a cluster of reader annotations with a non-empty 50\% reader-consensus mask, and its label is the median malignancy rating; benign is below 3 and malignant above 3. Of 2138 nodules from 717 patients (725 scans), the 747 with median 3 are excluded, leaving 1391 (937 benign, 454 malignant) from 611 patients (619 scans). Folds are assigned by patient with stratified five-fold splitting on each patient's modal grade (seed 42). A related study [BLINDED: companion paper reference] uses the same patient folds, on 1366 of these 1391 nodules (those with an unambiguous radiomics key), to compare radiomics--CNN fusion strategies; per-backbone AUCs are not identical across the two studies, and no result is shared.

\subsection{Backbones and Training}\label{sec:training}
Each input is the three central axial slices of a $64\times64$ crop (1 mm voxels, 64 mm field of view) centered on the nodule, with Hounsfield units clipped to $[-1000,400]$ and rescaled to $[0,1]$. Twelve ImageNet-pretrained backbones are used, drawn mostly from the families reviewed for lung CT in \cite{abdullahi_review_2025}: MobileNetV3-Small, EfficientNet-B0, ResNet50, and VGG16 at the native 64 px; DenseNet121, DenseNet201, GoogLeNet, InceptionV3, Inception-ResNet-v2, Xception, and ConvNeXt-Tiny \cite{liu_convnext_2022} resized to 96 px inside the model; and ViT-Base, a vision transformer, resized to 224 px. Resizing changes feature-map sizes, not the field of view. All backbones share one recipe: AdamW (convergence analyzed in \cite{zhou_adamw_2024}), learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, cosine schedule, batch size 16, at most 50 epochs with early stopping on validation AUC (patience 10), and random flips and rotations up to $\pm15^\circ$. Their mean five-fold validation areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) at the selected epoch (not nested) on the 1391 nodules range from 0.830 (MobileNetV3-Small) to 0.910 (VGG16); the seven 96 px backbones lie between 0.891 and 0.906.

\subsection{Localization Protocol}\label{sec:loc}
Maps are computed with Layer-CAM (pytorch-grad-cam 1.5.3) for the class the model predicts, on 60 nodules sampled once (seed 42) from fold 0 (21 labeled malignant, 39 benign) from 46 patients (35 contribute one nodule, 8 two, and 3 three), the same for all twelve backbones, using the checkpoint trained on folds 1--4. Its epoch maximizes AUC on fold 0, so fold 0 is held out from gradient updates but not from model selection. The reference is the 50\% reader-consensus mask of the central slice; maps are upsampled to it and min-max normalized. The main metric is pointing accuracy, the fraction of nodules whose map maximum falls inside the mask (ties broken by the first index in row-major order), as used in \cite{katar_transformer_2026}. Where it explains a failure we also report the Dice similarity coefficient $2|M\cap S|/(|M|+|S|)$ between the mask $M$ and the region $S$ above the 80th percentile of the map, and Dice with $|S|=|M|$ (size-matched). A map whose maximum falls uniformly at random scores the mean lesion area fraction, 0.019. Because every crop is centered on its nodule, we also score four model-free maps on the same 60 nodules and with the same metric code: a constant map, a map of i.i.d.\ uniform noise, an isotropic Gaussian centered on the crop, and a single bright pixel at the crop center. Pointing accuracies carry Wilson 95\% intervals.

\subsection{Explanation-Site Sweep and Selectors}\label{sec:sweep}
For each backbone we record every module that emits a spatial tensor in one forward pass and keep the last module at each output resolution and, where the band-rule module (below) is a different module, that module as well, giving 6 to 8 candidate sites per CNN and the 12 encoder blocks of ViT-Base, 86 in total. These 86 sites are the \emph{candidate set}. Modules called more than once and branches inside an already collapsed block are excluded; collapsed $1\times1$ sites are kept. Layer-CAM is recomputed at every site with the same weights, samples, and metric code.

Two automatic selectors are evaluated: the \emph{canonical} rule, which takes the deepest module with spatial extent, and a \emph{band} rule from our earlier pipeline, which takes the deepest module whose feature-map height lies in $[7,10]$ and otherwise falls back to a fixed per-architecture layer. We did not test the defaults of public CAM libraries. The canonical rule does not apply to ViT's token grid, where the selector falls back to the last encoder block. For each site we count the samples whose map is identically zero.

\subsection{Statistics}\label{sec:stats}
Statistical reporting follows the Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging (CLAIM) \cite{tejani_claim_2024}. Best-versus-canonical differences are tested with the exact McNemar test on per-nodule hits for the eight backbones whose best and canonical sites are different modules (membership is fixed by the site sets, not by the outcome; in the other four the two coincide), with Holm correction over those eight, and given percentile bootstrap 95\% intervals ($B=10{,}000$); because 14 of the 46 patients contribute more than one nodule, we also resample patients instead of nodules. Because the best site is chosen among 6 to 12 candidates per backbone on the same 60 nodules, these $p$-values are descriptive, conditional on that choice; we repeat the selection on a random half and score the selected and canonical sites on the other half, over 2000 splits, which gives an effect size free of that selection.

To examine the geometry of the pointing metric, we tabulate mean pointing accuracy by feature-map size and parity over the candidate sites of the eleven CNNs. For the GoogLeNet failure we cross-check the CAM method (Layer-CAM, HiResCAM \cite{draelos_hirescam_2021}, Grad-CAM \cite{santos_caveats_2025}, and Score-CAM, which scores masked copies of the input instead of using gradients \cite{suri_covlias_2022,taimeskhanov_walls_2024}) and run cascading weight randomization, as applied to medical saliency in \cite{arun_trustworthiness_2021}. The center-map baseline, the paired tests, the split-half check, the parity tabulation, and the two figures were added after the initial analysis.

\subsection{Implementation}\label{sec:impl}
All runs use seed 42 for the Python, NumPy, and PyTorch generators with deterministic cuDNN (no deterministic-algorithm flag, so runs are not guaranteed bit-exact), single-process data loading, and a patient-grouped split frozen on disk. The split-half and patient-level resampling seeds are fixed (20261011 and 20261022). Software: Python 3.11.9, PyTorch 2.3.0 (CUDA 12.1), and torchvision 0.18.0. Experiments ran on a computer with one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU (12 GB) under Windows 10.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{A Degenerate Explanation Site}\label{sec:degenerate}
Pointing accuracies in this section and the next are read against the center-map floor of Section~\ref{sec:centre}. A band rule from our pipeline resolved GoogLeNet to a $1\times1$ \texttt{Dropout}, and its score of 0.000 reflects the site, not the network. At this input GoogLeNet emits feature maps of heights $\{48,24,12,6,3,1\}$, none in $[7,10]$, so the rule fell back to \texttt{features.17}. A $1\times1$ activation upsampled to the input is constant and, after min-max normalization, identically zero on all 60 samples. Thresholding an all-zero map selects the whole frame $I$, so Dice equals $2|M|/(|M|+|I|)$, which averages 0.035 over the 60 samples here, and pointing accuracy is 0.000. No step raises an error.

Under the same weights, pointing accuracy is nonzero at every site with spatial extent but varies from 0.083 to 0.767 (Fig.~\ref{fig:diagnosis}): 0.500 at \texttt{features.15} ($3\times3$), 0.767 at \texttt{features.13} (max pooling, $3\times3$), 0.083 at \texttt{features.12} ($6\times6$), and 0.400 at \texttt{features.3} ($24\times24$). The \texttt{features.13} site is not in the candidate set, so the best-site values of the depth sweep (Section~\ref{sec:depthsweep}) are lower bounds. At \texttt{features.13} the other three methods also give nonzero scores: HiResCAM 0.417, Grad-CAM 0.383, and Score-CAM 0.417.

\subsection{A Center Map Outscores Every CAM}\label{sec:centre}
A center map, whose peak lies at the crop center on every nodule, scores 0.983 (59 of 60 nodules; Wilson 95\% CI 0.911--0.997), and so does a single bright center pixel (Table~\ref{tab:centre}). No Layer-CAM score from any of the 86 sites reaches this: the best is 0.733 (Inception-ResNet-v2, interval 0.610--0.829), and the best canonical site is 0.717 (ConvNeXt-Tiny, 0.592--0.815). The same ordering holds for size-matched Dice: 0.782 for the Gaussian center map against 0.444 for the best canonical site, 0.402 at the site with the best pointing accuracy, and at most 0.467 at any site (a different site). A constant map scores 0.000 and a uniform-noise map 0.033 (2 of 60), consistent with the 0.019 area fraction. Because the lesion lies near the center of every crop by construction, pointing accuracy here mostly measures how far a map's peak departs from the crop center, and every CAM departs from it more often than the lesion does. The site effects below remain differences between scores, but a score under this baseline cannot be read as evidence that the model localizes the lesion.

\subsection{The Depth Sweep}\label{sec:depthsweep}
Within the candidate set, the canonical site is the best in only four of twelve backbones (Fig.~\ref{fig:summary}a, Table~\ref{tab:depth}). Both show $\Delta$, best minus canonical, with a nodule-level bootstrap 95\% interval; the table's $p$ is the exact McNemar test, unadjusted, and the figure's asterisk marks Holm-adjusted $p<0.005$ (descriptive). In every CNN the deepest candidate is a collapsed $1\times1$ site, so the informative reference is the deepest site with spatial extent, the canonical one. The best site coincides with it in DenseNet121, DenseNet201, GoogLeNet, and ConvNeXt-Tiny (for GoogLeNet only within the candidate set) and is shallower in the other eight; in no backbone is a site deeper than the canonical one the best. By shape, nine curves peak in the middle (including the four that peak at the canonical site), two at their shallowest candidate (ResNet50 and VGG16), and one is flat (MobileNetV3-Small, range 0.033).

The gap between best and canonical site is significant in six of the eight backbones in which the two sites differ, from 0.183 in EfficientNet-B0 to 0.633 in Inception-ResNet-v2 (exact McNemar $p\leq9.8\times10^{-4}$, Holm-adjusted $p\leq2.9\times10^{-3}$, bootstrap intervals excluding zero; descriptive, since the best site is chosen on the same nodules), and not in MobileNetV3-Small ($p=0.50$) or ViT-Base ($p=0.13$). Resampling the 46 patients instead of the 60 nodules leaves all six gaps above zero, with lower 95\% bounds from 0.09 (EfficientNet-B0) to 0.47 (Inception-ResNet-v2); the patient-level interval is also above zero for ViT-Base (0.02 to 0.14) although the exact test does not reject, and reaches zero for MobileNetV3-Small. Choosing the site on 30 nodules and scoring it on the other 30 leaves the six gaps at 0.180 to 0.631, with the 2.5th percentile above zero for all six, so selection on the same nodules inflates them by at most 0.056. Ranking the twelve backbones by canonical score or by best-site score gives similar but not identical orders (Spearman 0.82, computed from Table~\ref{tab:depth}), and the leader changes from ConvNeXt-Tiny to Inception-ResNet-v2, which moves up from fifth.

Part of the site effect may be geometric (Fig.~\ref{fig:summary}b). A map with $n\times n$ cells has a middle cell centered on the crop center only if $n$ is odd; for even $n$ the cell centers nearest the center lie $32/n$ px away on the 64 px crop (16, 8, and 5.3 px for $n=2,4,6$), while the mean lesion, 1.9\% of the frame, has an equivalent radius of about 5 px. A map whose peak sits at a cell center can then hit a centered lesion only when $n$ is odd or the lesion is large.

Fig.~\ref{fig:summary}b plots the 63 CNN sites with a spatial map (ViT-Base excluded) by feature-map size, with stars at each backbone's canonical site and lines at the mean over sites of at most six cells. The scores are consistent with this pattern but cannot prove it: at $n\leq6$, odd-sized maps (all $3\times3$) average 0.498 over seven sites, which come from only five backbones (ConvNeXt-Tiny and Xception each contribute two $3\times3$ sites), and even-sized maps ($n=2,4,6$: 0.000, 0.033, 0.093) average 0.044 over fifteen; over all sites with $n>1$ the means are 0.380 (16 sites) and 0.194 (47 sites). All four backbones with a $2\times2$ canonical site score 0.000 there. The four canonical sites that are best are all $3\times3$, and every other canonical site that fails, except Xception's, is even-sized. On centered crops, explanation quality, depth, and grid geometry cannot be separated.

\subsection{Silent-Failure Census}\label{sec:census}
Seventeen of the 86 sites emit an identically-zero map on at least one sample and 12 on all 60, and every backbone has at least one such site. Eleven of the twelve all-sample failures are $1\times1$ modules (adaptive average pools in nine backbones and dropouts in GoogLeNet and InceptionV3), each returning a mean Dice of 0.035 with pointing accuracy 0.000. The twelfth, a $4\times4$ SiLU in EfficientNet-B0, has spatial extent, so a size check alone would not catch the failure. The five partial failures (Xception, MobileNetV3-Small, and three ViT-Base blocks, zero on 9 to 53 samples) return finite Dice between 0.036 and 0.072. The band rule lands on an affected site in three backbones (GoogLeNet, ViT-Base, Xception) and the canonical rule in one (ViT-Base).

\subsection{The Training Protocol Changes the Canonical Score}\label{sec:protocol}
ConvNeXt-Tiny, DenseNet121, and DenseNet201 were retrained with the epoch chosen on an inner split of the training folds and a two-stage fine-tuning recipe. Scored with the same code on the same 60 fold-0 nodules, pointing accuracy at the unchanged canonical site falls from 0.717 to 0.500, 0.583 to 0.250, and 0.700 to 0.000, respectively (exact McNemar test on the paired hits: $p=0.015$, $0.00018$, and $4.5\times10^{-13}$, descriptive). The DenseNet201 drop is not a degenerate site: none of its maps is identically zero, and its pointing accuracy is 0.000 in all five fold checkpoints. In both DenseNets the best fold-0 site moves from the deepest spatial candidate to the shallowest (48$\times$48). In DenseNet201 it reaches 0.350 against 0.000 at the canonical site (21 nodules differ, all in one direction), but in DenseNet121 it reaches 0.417 against 0.250 (21 versus 11 nodules differ, $p=0.11$, with the site chosen on the same nodules), so only the DenseNet201 shift is clearly beyond selection on the sample. Each protocol is one run and two factors changed (epoch selection and fine-tuning recipe), so the shift cannot be attributed to either or separated from seed variation; a best site fixed once per architecture can be wrong after retraining.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The center map comes first, because it decides how every pointing score is read. It wins because every crop is centered on its nodule, so a prior that needs no model reaches 0.983. This does not show that CAMs localize poorly in general; it shows that, on centered crops, a pointing score below 0.98 is no evidence that a model uses the lesion, and that a CAM score is interpretable only beside this floor. A baseline of this kind, an average mask, has been used on chest radiographs \cite{arun_trustworthiness_2021}.

The site effects themselves come from how selectors choose a layer. Neither rule inspects the map it is about to produce: the canonical rule stops at the deepest module with a spatial shape, which at a 64 px input can be $2\times2$, and the band rule's $[7,10]$ interval, suited to a 224 px input, can be empty on small crops, so the fallback lands on a pooling or dropout layer.

These findings bear on decision support. A clinician shown a CAM is shown one site's view of the network. Because the score changes with the site, the training protocol, and the grid geometry, we suggest four steps. Resolve the site by name and report its map size. Reject constant and $1\times1$ maps and report the zero-map count. When crops are centered on the lesion, score a center map on the same samples and report it beside the CAM score. Select the site on data separate from the data used to report it.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
Every crop is centered on its nodule, so the center map is strong because of how the data were prepared; off-center crops were not evaluated. The parity analysis is descriptive and rests on seven odd-sized sites from five backbones, all $3\times3$, and the scores describe where the maps peak, not whether the model relies on the lesion. Localization also uses only 60 nodules from one fold (46 patients, 14 of whom contribute more than one nodule, so the exact McNemar test, which treats nodules as independent, is optimistic; patient-level intervals are given for the best-versus-canonical gaps). The split-half check bounds but does not remove selection inflation, one checkpoint per backbone was used, training-seed variation was not measured, and the fold-0 checkpoint epoch was also chosen on fold 0.

Maps explain the class the model predicts, which for 45 of 60 GoogLeNet predictions is benign; we did not compare with maps for a fixed class. Each map explains the predicted class whether or not it is correct: on these 60 nodules accuracy ranges from 0.67 to 0.90 and malignant recall from 0.43 to 0.76 across the twelve backbones (Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion60}: 39 benign and 21 malignant nodules, scored at the predicted class; the counts describe the explained set, not classifier performance). The maps are also only weakly decision-dependent: cascading randomization of GoogLeNet at \texttt{features.13} leaves rank correlations of 0.85 to 0.71 until mid-level stages are randomized, and the non-predicted-class map correlates 0.77 with the original.

Between-backbone comparisons are also confounded: the four 64 px backbones and ViT-Base are those with a canonical score of 0.000, so differences between backbones cannot be separated from feature-map resolution; within-backbone curves are unaffected. One dataset, one CAM method for the sweep, one candidate per resolution plus the band-rule module, and one training recipe were used; ViT-Base's CAM path is degenerate and says nothing about ViT interpretability; and the protocol effect rests on three backbones and single runs.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
On centered crops of LIDC-IDRI nodules, a parameter-free center map scored 0.983 in pointing accuracy, above every Layer-CAM score from every one of 86 sites across twelve backbones (maximum 0.733), so these scores do not show that a model localizes the lesion. The scores nonetheless depend strongly on the site: the canonical last-spatial-layer site was the best candidate in only four backbones, a shallower site beat it by 0.18 to 0.63 in six even when selected and scored on separate nodules, and the scores are consistent with an effect of map-grid parity. The canonical score also fell when three backbones were retrained with a different protocol, and automatic site selection can return a constant map that still yields a finite score. A CAM score should be reported with its site, the site's spatial size, its count of zero maps, and, for centered crops, a center-map baseline.
