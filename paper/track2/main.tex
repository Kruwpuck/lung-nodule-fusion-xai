\begin{abstract}
A backbone comparison is usually read as a question about architecture, but three design axes move together in one: model capacity, the training protocol, and the architecture family itself. We ask which of the three governs performance and interpretability for lung nodule malignancy classification on LIDC-IDRI, and find that the decisive variable is none of them. It is the \emph{explanation site}: the layer at which a class-activation map is taken. Sweeping 86 candidate sites across twelve backbones, pointing accuracy against the radiologist mask moves by up to 0.7333 within a single fixed network as the site is varied, which is larger than any between-backbone difference we measure at a fixed site. The canonical last-spatial-layer choice is best in only 4 of 12 backbones, and in none of the twelve is the deepest candidate site the best one. Automatic target-layer selectors fail silently on this task: of the 86 sites, 17 emit an identically-zero map on at least one sample and 12 do so on every sample, and because a constant-zero map still yields a finite Dice value (0.0350) and a finite pointing rate (0.0000), a selector that lands there reports a plausible number instead of an error. One published row of ours was produced this way: GoogLeNet scores 0.0000 pointing accuracy at the automatically resolved site, a $1\times1$ dropout layer with no spatial extent, and 0.7667 at a $3\times3$ site in the same network under the same weights. Capacity is a subordinate confirmation rather than a discovery: over seven backbones sharing a 96\,px input, a 9.7-fold parameter range (5.60 to 54.31\,M) spans only 0.0144 AUC (0.8911 to 0.9055), and the 27.82\,M model attains the highest AUC of the seven while the 54.31\,M model does not; the Spearman correlation between capacity and AUC is 0.50 at $n=7$ and is not significant ($p=0.25$), which at this sample size settles the capacity question in neither direction. On the protocol axis, a factorial ANOVA over four backbones attributes $\eta^2=0.4533$ of AUC variance to optimizer choice against $0.0002$ to weight decay; a follow-up learning-rate sweep confirms this is a learning-rate-mismatch artifact rather than an intrinsic optimizer ranking (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). The practical consequence is that any cross-architecture explainability number reported without a target-layer audit is uninterpretable, and may be an artifact of the tooling rather than a property of the network.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A comparison of convolutional backbones on a medical imaging task is normally presented as an architecture study, but at least three variables move at once when the backbone is swapped. Capacity changes, because families differ by an order of magnitude in parameter count. The training protocol interacts with the architecture, because a single learning rate and optimizer cannot suit every family equally. And the architecture family itself changes, bringing a different stem, a different downsampling schedule, and a different classification head. If a comparison reports one accuracy number per backbone, none of the three can be separated from the others. This paper asks the umbrella question directly: among capacity, optimizer, and architecture family, which governs performance and which governs interpretability?

Our answer is that for interpretability the governing variable is not on that list. It is the layer at which the explanation is computed. We sweep 86 candidate explanation sites across twelve backbones and measure Layer-CAM localization against radiologist nodule masks at each. Within a single frozen network, pointing accuracy varies by as much as 0.7333 across sites. That within-model range exceeds every between-model difference we observe at a fixed site, which reverses the usual reading of a cross-architecture explainability table: the table is largely reporting where the maps were taken, not which network produced them.

This matters beyond a methodological footnote because the site is usually chosen automatically. Explainability tooling resolves a target layer by a heuristic -- the last convolutional module, or the deepest module whose feature map falls in some size band -- and that heuristic can land on a module with no spatial extent at all. A $1\times1$ activation upsampled to the input produces a constant map. A constant map is not an error: it flows through the metric code and produces a finite Dice value and a finite pointing rate. The failure is therefore silent, and the number it produces is in the same range as a real one. We find 17 such sites among the 86, and one of them sits under a row we ourselves previously published.

The capacity axis behaves as the literature would predict, and we report it as a domain confirmation rather than a finding. On the seven backbones that share a 96\,px input, a 9.7-fold spread in parameters buys 0.0144 AUC, and the largest model is not the best one. The optimizer axis absorbs our earlier stability and label-granularity analyses: optimizer choice dominates weight decay in a variance decomposition by three orders of magnitude in $\eta^2$, so the protocol lever that matters is not the one usually swept.

The contribution is methodological and actionable. First, we show that explanation site, not architecture family or capacity, is the dominant source of variation in cross-backbone CAM localization on this task, and we quantify it with a per-backbone depth sweep in which each curve is its own control. Second, we census the silent-failure mode of automatic target-layer selection, give its rate over 86 sites, and show it is silent by construction because degenerate maps still produce finite metrics. Third, we diagnose one affected backbone in full, with a layer sweep, a cross-method check, a cascading weight-randomization test, and a class-flip probe, and report where that diagnosis stops short of a faithfulness claim. Fourth, we report the capacity and optimizer axes on the same cohort, with their subset memberships made explicit, so that the three claims are not silently read as resting on the same models.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Capacity on small medical cohorts.} That additional parameters buy little accuracy on medical imaging datasets of this scale is established rather than novel. The canonical result attributes much of the apparent transfer benefit of large ImageNet architectures to over-parameterization rather than to sophisticated feature reuse, and finds lightweight models competitive with them on medical tasks \cite{raghu_transfusion_2019}. The efficiency framing itself -- parameters, FLOPs, latency, memory, and accuracy-per-parameter, with a Pareto plot -- is a settled convention \cite{schwartz_greenai_2020,strubell_energy_2019}. We therefore present our capacity result as a confirmation on a new cohort, not as a discovery. We are also explicit that we could not locate a single peer-reviewed source reporting LIDC-IDRI classification AUC alongside parameter counts in one table; the precedent is assembled from partial sources.

\textbf{Cross-architecture CAM comparison.} Prior cross-architecture CAM comparisons in medical imaging typically fix a single explanation site per model and compare overlap statistics across architectures. This work departs from that pattern along several axes at once: we compare twelve backbones spanning several CNN families plus a ViT rather than two or three, we report the pointing game and Dice rather than custom overlap statistics, and -- the substantive departure -- we move from a single fixed site per model to a sweep over every candidate site, which is what lets us attribute the variation to the site rather than to the family. To our knowledge, no prior work on lung nodule CT reports a per-backbone explanation-site sweep alongside parameter efficiency on a common cohort, but we state that as a hedged claim about our search, not as an absolute.

\textbf{Layer choice and the mechanism of failure.} The dependence of localization on layer depth is documented in the primary sources: the original Grad-CAM work observes that localization degrades toward earlier convolutional layers and that final-layer maps are coarse \cite{selvaraju_gradcam_2017}, and Layer-CAM was designed precisely to recover fine localization from higher-resolution intermediate layers \cite{jiang_layercam_2021}. What follows from those two facts, and what we did not find stated, is that the canonical last-layer recommendation is a default rather than an optimum, and that an automatic implementation of it can degrade into a site with no spatial extent while continuing to emit metrics. We are not aware of a source for the silent-selection claim itself, and mark it as such.

\textbf{Faithfulness is a separate question from localization.} Overlap with a mask measures where a map is bright, not whether the model used that region. Gradient-averaging CAMs are argued to be provably faithful only for heads of the gap-then-single-linear-layer form, which several of our backbones do not have \cite{draelos_hirescam_2020}, and the standard diagnostic is cascading parameter randomization \cite{adebayo_sanitychecks_2018}. We run that diagnostic and report, in Section~\ref{sec:limits}, that our maps only partly pass it. A gradient-free method provides an independent cross-check \cite{wang_scorecam_2020}, and the pointing metric we use originates in the excitation-backprop literature \cite{zhang_excitationbackprop_2018}.

\textbf{Benchmarking with few models.} Twelve architectures and seven cost-profiled ones are too few to support a powered claim about families, and the benchmarking-variance literature argues for reporting variance and withholding strong rankings under such conditions \cite{bouthillier_variance_2021}. We adopt that posture throughout. Comparative work in this domain more often varies modality, feature type, and architecture together in a single pipeline comparison \cite{prabhavalkar_hybrid_2026} than it isolates one design axis, which is the gap in method rather than in topic that we try to address.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

The dataset, split, preprocessing, and label definitions below are stated in full. A companion paper on radiomics-CNN fusion uses the same cohort and the same fold assignment, and each paper must be readable without the other; restating the cohort here is deliberate and is not an independent contribution of either paper.

\subsection{Dataset}\label{sec:dataset}
We use LIDC-IDRI \cite{armato_lidcidri_2011}. The cohort comprises 717 patients and 725 scans, with reconstructed slice thickness from 0.45 to 5.0\,mm (median 1.25\,mm). After per-nodule aggregation, 2138 nodule rows remain, with a median 3-D maximum diameter of 11.0\,mm (range 2.45 to 54.28\,mm).

\subsection{Labels}\label{sec:labels}
Each nodule carries malignancy ratings on the 1 to 5 scale from up to four readers; the nodule label is the median of those ratings. We define benign as a median below 3, malignant as a median above 3, and indeterminate as a median of exactly 3. Four label arms are trained. The \emph{binary} arm drops the 747 indeterminate nodules and retains 1391 (937 benign, 454 malignant). The \emph{ordinal} arm keeps the full 1 to 5 scale (260/677/747/353/101 nodules at ratings 1 through 5). The \emph{three-class} arm keeps all 2138 nodules with indeterminate as its own class. The \emph{four-class} arm adds 2138 hard negatives drawn from LUNA16 class-0 candidates as a no-nodule class, for 4276 rows. Unless stated otherwise, every AUC in this paper is a benign-versus-malignant AUC on the 1391-nodule binary-eligible set.

\subsection{Split and Preprocessing}\label{sec:split}
The cohort is partitioned into five folds by patient identifier with seed 42, so no patient contributes to more than one fold, and every backbone and every arm uses the identical assignment. Each nodule is represented as a 2.5-D patch: a stack of adjacent axial slices centered on the nodule, resampled and cropped to a fixed in-plane size. Hounsfield units are clipped to $[-1000, 400]$ and rescaled to $[0,1]$.

\subsection{Backbone Set and the Three Subsets}\label{sec:subsets}
The three axes of this paper do not rest on the same models, and the subsets are not strictly nested. We name them once and carry the names through the results.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{localization12} -- twelve backbones with Layer-CAM measurements: MobileNetV3-Small \cite{howard_mobilenetv3_2019}, EfficientNet-B0 \cite{tan_efficientnet_2019}, ResNet50 \cite{he_resnet_2016}, VGG16 \cite{simonyan_vgg_2015} (64\,px input), DenseNet121, DenseNet201 \cite{huang_densenet_2017}, GoogLeNet \cite{szegedy_googlenet_2015}, InceptionV3 \cite{szegedy_inceptionv3_2016}, Inception-ResNet-v2 \cite{szegedy_inceptionresnet_2017}, Xception \cite{chollet_xception_2017}, ConvNeXt-Tiny \cite{liu_convnext_2022} (96\,px), and ViT-Base \cite{dosovitskiy_vit_2021} (224\,px).
\item \textbf{efficiency7} -- the seven of those twelve that run at a shared 96\,px input. Only these carry cost measurements, because GFLOPs, latency, and memory all scale with input size and mixing three input sizes in one cost column would invite exactly the comparison the column exists to support.
\item \textbf{optimizer4} -- MobileNetV2 \cite{sandler_mobilenetv2_2018}, EfficientNet-B0, ResNet50, VGG16. MobileNetV2 appears in no other subset, so optimizer4 is \emph{not} a subset of localization12.
\end{itemize}
Input resolution is treated here as an architectural variable rather than a nuisance parameter, because several of the twelve cannot accept a common input without upsampling that would itself alter the maps. Section~\ref{sec:limits} states what that costs us.

\subsection{Efficiency Measurement}\label{sec:efficiency-method}
For the seven backbones at 96\,px we report parameter count, GFLOPs per forward pass, median CPU latency with interquartile range, median GPU latency, and peak GPU memory. Latency is wall-clock and warm-started, at batch size 1, over 7 repeats of 50 forward passes each; the median over repeats is reported. Measurements were taken on an Intel Core CPU (family 6, model 151) and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 under PyTorch 2.3.0 with CUDA 12.1 on Windows. We also report accuracy per million parameters and a Pareto front over accuracy against GFLOPs. FLOPs is a poor proxy for wall-clock latency, and both are hardware- and batch-size-dependent, so the two cost columns are read together rather than either alone.

\subsection{Localization Protocol}\label{sec:localization-protocol}
Class-activation maps are computed with Layer-CAM \cite{jiang_layercam_2021} on a fixed set of 60 fold-0 nodules with radiologist-annotated masks, identical across all twelve backbones. Maps are upsampled to the input grid, min-max normalized, and thresholded at the 80th percentile of map energy for the overlap metrics. We report Dice, IoU, a size-matched Dice variant that removes the dependence of raw Dice on the ratio between map area and lesion area, a pointing-game hit rate \cite{zhang_excitationbackprop_2018} recording whether the map's maximum falls inside the mask, and an energy-based pointing metric giving the fraction of map energy inside the mask \cite{wang_scorecam_2020}. Chance pointing accuracy is 0.0189, the mean lesion area fraction over the same 60 samples; a pointing rate near that value indicates a map carrying no localization information.

\subsection{Explanation Site: the Sweep and the Two Selectors}\label{sec:sitesel}
For each backbone we enumerate every module that emits a spatial activation and order the candidates from shallow to deep by normalized execution index, giving 86 candidate sites over the twelve backbones (6 to 12 per backbone; 12 for ViT-Base, whose blocks all emit a $14\times14$ token grid). Layer-CAM is recomputed at each site with the same weights, the same 60 samples, and the same metrics, so a within-backbone curve of localization quality against depth is its own control: nothing but the site changes along it.

Against those curves we evaluate the two automatic rules we have used in practice. The \emph{canonical} rule takes the deepest module with spatial extent, the last-convolutional-layer default the CAM literature recommends \cite{selvaraju_gradcam_2017}. The \emph{band} rule takes the deepest module whose feature-map height falls in $[7,10]$, a heuristic intended to trade resolution against semantic depth. Both are resolved programmatically from the module graph, which is how a degenerate site can be selected without anyone noticing. We record, for every one of the 86 sites, how many of the 60 maps are identically zero, and whether the site is the canonical pick, the band pick, or the site behind our own previously published row.

\subsection{Diagnosis Protocol for a Suspected Degenerate Site}\label{sec:diagnosis-protocol}
A pointing accuracy of exactly zero is an extraordinary result and is treated as a suspected implementation artifact until excluded. For the affected backbone we run four checks. (i) A layer sweep, to establish whether any site in the same network under the same weights recovers localization above chance. (ii) A CAM-method cross-check at the recovered site, comparing Layer-CAM against HiResCAM, Grad-CAM, and the gradient-free Score-CAM \cite{wang_scorecam_2020,draelos_hirescam_2020}, to exclude a gradient-averaging artifact specific to that head. (iii) Cascading weight randomization from the classifier backwards, reporting the Spearman rank correlation between the original map and the map from the progressively randomized model \cite{adebayo_sanitychecks_2018}. (iv) A class-flip probe, computing the map for the non-predicted class and its rank correlation with the original. A verdict of \emph{artifact} requires all three of: the resolved site emits an all-zero map on every sample; some candidate site recovers pointing accuracy above chance plus two standard errors with no zero maps; and the recovered map's mean rank correlation with the fully randomized map falls below 0.5.

\subsection{Optimizer and Protocol Axis}\label{sec:optimizer-method}
On optimizer4 we train a full grid of 4 backbones $\times$ 3 optimizers (SGD with momentum 0.9, Adam, AdamW) $\times$ 3 weight decays (the PyTorch default and one order of magnitude either side) $\times$ 5 folds, 45 runs per backbone. We decompose AUC variance with a factorial ANOVA over backbone, optimizer, weight decay, and fold, reporting $\eta^2$ per factor. Stability is tested in variance rather than in point estimates: a Brown-Forsythe test (the median-based Levene variant, for robustness to skew) on AUC variance for each of the six backbone pairs, with Holm correction across that family.

The label scheme is a second protocol lever. Each of the four label arms is collapsed to a common benign-versus-malignant probability by renormalization rather than by argmax, which preserves the continuous score AUC requires. The binary arm uses its malignant probability directly; the ordinal arm uses the cumulative probability of a rating above 3; the three-class arm uses $P(\text{malignant})/(P(\text{benign})+P(\text{malignant}))$; the four-class arm is first restricted to the nodule subset and then renormalized identically, which guards against the AUC inflation that a large pool of easy true negatives would produce. All arms are scored on the identical 1391 binary-eligible nodules per fold. Paired DeLong tests compare arm pairs on that common subset, valid here specifically because the arms share cases, and a Friedman test with Nemenyi post-hoc over per-fold collapsed-binary AUC gives an omnibus ranking that does not require the shared-cases assumption. For the ordinal arm we additionally report quadratic-weighted kappa, mean absolute error, and one-off accuracy on the native 1 to 5 scale.

\subsection{Statistical Posture}\label{sec:statistical-posture}
Twelve models and seven cost-profiled models are small samples. Correlations are reported as descriptive summaries with $n$ attached, never as inferential support for a mechanism. Pointing-accuracy intervals are Wilson score intervals at 95\% over 60 Bernoulli trials, chosen because several backbones score exactly zero and the normal approximation degenerates there; Dice intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals at 95\% over the 60 per-sample values with $B=10{,}000$ and seed 20260820. This study is observational and hypothesis-generating throughout.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{A Degenerate Explanation Site, Dissected}\label{sec:degenerate}
One row of our own earlier localization table reported a pointing accuracy of exactly 0.0000 for GoogLeNet, together with a Dice of 0.0350, an IoU of 0.0189, a size-matched Dice of 0.0113, and a mean inside-mask energy of 0.0000. Following Section~\ref{sec:diagnosis-protocol}, that row was treated as a suspected artifact rather than as a property of the network, and the diagnosis is conclusive.

The site had been resolved by the band rule. GoogLeNet at a 96\,px input emits feature maps of heights $\{48, 24, 12, 6, 3, 1\}$; none of these falls inside the $[7,10]$ band, so the rule exhausted its candidates and fell back to the deepest module in the graph. That module is \texttt{features.17}, a \texttt{Dropout} with a $1\times1$ output. A $1\times1$ activation has no spatial structure to weight, so Layer-CAM returns a constant map, and after min-max normalization a constant map is identically zero on all 60 samples. Thresholding an all-zero map at the 80th percentile selects the whole frame, which is why the metrics remain finite: Dice against a small lesion mask becomes $2|M|/(|M|+|I|)$, a fixed 0.0350 on this sample set, while the pointing rate collapses to 0.0000 because the argmax of a constant map is not inside the mask by any tie-break. Nothing in this chain raises an error. The immediately preceding module, \texttt{features.16}, is an adaptive average pool, also $1\times1$, and produces exactly the same numbers.

Localization recovers as soon as the map is taken somewhere with spatial extent, under identical weights. Fig.~\ref{fig:diagnosis} shows the layer sweep. At \texttt{features.15} (\texttt{inception5b}, $3\times3$) pointing accuracy is 0.5000 and Dice 0.0901; at \texttt{features.13} (\texttt{maxpool4}, $3\times3$) it reaches 0.7667 with Dice 0.1216 and size-matched Dice 0.5262; at \texttt{features.12} ($6\times6$) it falls to 0.0833, and at \texttt{features.3} ($24\times24$) it is 0.4000. Chance pointing accuracy on these 60 samples, the mean lesion area fraction, is 0.0189. A cross-check at \texttt{features.13} excludes a gradient-averaging artifact peculiar to Layer-CAM: HiResCAM reaches 0.4167, Grad-CAM 0.3833, and the gradient-free Score-CAM 0.4167, all far above chance, at 0.5329\,s per sample for Score-CAM against 0.0253\,s for Layer-CAM. The published 0.0000 was therefore a property of where the map was taken and not of the network, of the weights, or of the CAM family.

\subsection{The Depth Sweep: the Site Dominates}\label{sec:depthsweep}
Fig.~\ref{fig:depthsweep} and Table~\ref{tab:depth} give the sweep over all 86 candidate sites, 6 to 12 per backbone. Every curve is a within-model control: only the site changes along it.

Curve shapes are assigned by a fixed rule: sites are ordered shallow to deep by normalized execution index, scored by pointing accuracy, and classified as flat if the max-min range is below 0.05 (three of sixty samples), shallow-peaked if the best site is the first, deep-peaked if it is the last, and mid-peaked otherwise. Nine of the twelve curves are mid-peaked, two are shallow-peaked (ResNet50 and VGG16, both best at their shallowest candidate), and one is flat (MobileNetV3-Small, whose whole range is 0.0333). \textbf{No curve is deep-peaked.} In none of the twelve backbones is the deepest candidate site the best one, which is a direct empirical contradiction of the last-convolutional-layer default as an optimum rather than as a convention.

The canonical rule is best in only 4 of 12 backbones (DenseNet121, GoogLeNet, ConvNeXt-Tiny, DenseNet201), and in each of those four it coincides with the best site rather than beating it. The largest gap between a backbone's best site and the canonical pick is 0.6333, in Inception-ResNet-v2: 0.7333 at a $20\times20$ site against 0.1000 at the $4\times4$ canonical pick. VGG16 gives 0.4667 against 0.0000, ResNet50 0.3833 against 0.0000, and InceptionV3 0.4333 against 0.0333. The band rule fails differently: its largest deficit is 0.5000 in GoogLeNet, where it lands on the degenerate $1\times1$ site of Section~\ref{sec:degenerate}, and it is also 0.2667 behind on ResNet50 and VGG16 and 0.3500 behind on Inception-ResNet-v2. Taken over the twelve, the within-backbone range in pointing accuracy runs up to 0.7333 (Inception-ResNet-v2), against a between-backbone spread at the published sites of 0.7167 (Table~\ref{tab:joint}); the site moves the metric at least as far as the choice of network does.

\subsection{Silent-Failure Census}\label{sec:census}
The GoogLeNet row is not an isolated accident. Of the 86 candidate sites, 17 emit an identically-zero map on at least one of the 60 samples, and 12 do so on every sample. At least one affected site occurs in \emph{all twelve} backbones, so the failure mode is a property of how explanation sites are enumerated on this task and not of one architecture.

Eleven of the twelve all-sample failures are $1\times1$ modules -- adaptive average pools in eight backbones, dropouts in GoogLeNet and InceptionV3 -- where the constant-map mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:degenerate} applies exactly, and each of them emits the same Dice of 0.0350 with a pointing rate of 0.0000. The twelfth has real spatial extent: EfficientNet-B0 at \texttt{features.0.6.0.block.0.2}, a $4\times4$ SiLU, is identically zero on all 60 samples, which shows that a size check alone would not catch the failure. The five partial failures span $3\times3$ to $14\times14$: Xception at \texttt{features.conv4} is zero on 9 of 60, MobileNetV3-Small at a $4\times4$ Hardswish on 49 of 60, and ViT-Base on 27, 50 and 53 of 60 at encoder layers 8, 10 and 11 respectively, a monotone worsening with depth. Every one of these emits a finite Dice, between 0.0357 and 0.0716.

The two selectors are hit at different rates. The band rule resolves to a site with at least one zero map in three backbones (GoogLeNet, ViT-Base, Xception); the canonical rule does so in one (ViT-Base). That one is a published row: the resolver placed ViT-Base at \texttt{encoder\_layer\_11.ln\_1}, zero on 53 of 60 samples, and the row still reported a finite Dice of 0.0357 and a pointing accuracy of 0.0000. A reader of that table has no signal distinguishing it from a network that simply localizes poorly.

\subsection{Sanity Checks at the Recovered Site}\label{sec:sanity}
Recovering a plausible map is not the same as recovering a faithful one, and the cascading-randomization test is explicit about the difference. Randomizing GoogLeNet from the classifier backwards, the Spearman rank correlation between the original map at \texttt{features.13} and the map from the progressively randomized model is 0.8500 with the classifier alone randomized, then 0.8015, 0.7671 and 0.7087 as \texttt{features.15}, \texttt{features.14} and \texttt{features.12} are added. It collapses to 0.0033 once \texttt{features.10} is randomized, and goes mildly negative thereafter ($-0.2659$, $-0.2967$, $-0.2550$ down to \texttt{features.0}). The map therefore does depend on the trained weights, but only on the mid-level ones; the deepest four stages can be destroyed with the explanation largely intact. The class-flip probe points the same way: the map computed for the non-predicted class correlates with the original at a mean Spearman of 0.7712 (median 0.7967), so the map is only weakly class-discriminative.

The pre-registered verdict criteria are all met -- the resolved site is all-zero on every sample, a candidate site recovers pointing accuracy far above chance with no zero maps, and the recovered map's correlation with the fully randomized map is below 0.5 -- so the automated verdict is \emph{artifact}. We record the sanity results as a bound on what the recovered map licenses, and return to them in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\subsection{Efficiency: Capacity Confirms Rather Than Discovers}\label{sec:efficiency}
On efficiency7, the seven backbones sharing a 96\,px input, parameters range from 5.60\,M (GoogLeNet) to 54.31\,M (Inception-ResNet-v2), a 9.7-fold spread. Across that entire range mean AUC moves from 0.8911 (Xception) to 0.9055 (ConvNeXt-Tiny), a spread of 0.0144. Six of the seven backbones have a within-model standard deviation across the five folds larger than that spread: the fold standard deviations run from 0.0119 (GoogLeNet) to 0.0283 (Xception), with only GoogLeNet's below 0.0144. The entire capacity effect over an order of magnitude in parameters is thus smaller than the run-to-run noise of a single model, which is the reason we decline to rank these seven on AUC.

The largest model is not the best one. The 27.82\,M ConvNeXt-Tiny attains the highest AUC of the seven; the 54.31\,M Inception-ResNet-v2 reaches 0.8986, fifth of seven, at 34.4\,ms median CPU latency and 346.8\,MB peak GPU memory, the worst on both cost axes. Accuracy per million parameters spans an order of magnitude in the opposite direction, from 0.1600 for GoogLeNet and 0.1285 for DenseNet121 down to 0.0165 for Inception-ResNet-v2. The Pareto front over AUC against GFLOPs, marked in Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto}, contains three models: \textbf{GoogLeNet} (0.5558 GFLOPs, 0.8962 AUC), \textbf{InceptionV3} (0.6828, 0.8992) and \textbf{ConvNeXt-Tiny} (1.6486, 0.9055). The remaining four are dominated. GFLOPs and wall-clock latency do not agree even within the front: InceptionV3 costs 23\% more GFLOPs than GoogLeNet but only 45\% more CPU time, while Xception at 1.6683 GFLOPs is the second-fastest of the seven on GPU.

Neither capacity correlation is significant. On efficiency7, Spearman between parameter count and mean AUC is $\rho=0.50$ at $n=7$ with $p=0.2532$. On localization12, with input resolution free to vary, Spearman between parameter count and pointing accuracy is $\rho=0.13$ at $n=12$ with $p=0.6860$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:pointingparams}). Both are reported as descriptive summaries of a small sample; neither demonstrates a monotone association, and neither is evidence that no association exists.

The gap is driven by SGD: at the single learning rate shared by all three optimizers, SGD costs EfficientNet-B0 0.146 AUC relative to Adam (0.7287 against 0.8744) and ResNet50 0.126 (0.7720 against 0.8980), while Adam and AdamW are within 0.006 of each other on every backbone. The label scheme is the second protocol lever. The Friedman omnibus test across the four label arms is significant ($\chi^2=44.52$, $p=1.2\times10^{-9}$), with mean ranks favoring the four-class arm (1.37) over binary (2.27), three-class (2.87) and ordinal (3.50, worst). The three-class arm is therefore the one arm that separates from neither of its neighbors, sitting between them with no gap this design can detect in either direction, while the four-class arm does separate from both ($p=0.0350$ against binary, $p<0.0001$ against three-class). Read as a statement about label granularity, that is the substantive result of this test: moving from two classes to three buys no detectable separation, and moving to four does.

At the individual-model level, paired DeLong tests for binary versus four-class reach significance in only 3 of the 6 models tested (MobileNetV3-Small, VGG16, ViT-Base), and are not significant for DenseNet121, EfficientNet-B0 or ResNet50 ($p=0.0644$ to $0.6846$). Label granularity therefore affects performance in aggregate across models and folds without holding uniformly at the level of an individual model. Ordinal-native metrics for the ordinal arm, averaged over its six models and five folds, are a quadratic-weighted kappa of 0.5451, a mean absolute error of 0.6433 and a one-off accuracy of 0.9155.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Why a selector lands where it does.} Neither rule inspects the signal it is about to explain; both walk the module graph and stop on a structural condition. The canonical rule stops at the deepest module that reports a spatial shape, and a module can report a shape without carrying spatial structure -- an adaptive average pool and a dropout after it both emit a well-formed $1\times1$ tensor. The band rule stops at the deepest module whose feature-map height lies in $[7,10]$, and that interval is not a property of the network at all. It is a property of a $224$\,px input, where an ImageNet backbone terminates at $7\times7$. Our patches are 96\,px and 64\,px, so the same GoogLeNet emits heights $\{48,24,12,6,3,1\}$ and the band is empty; the rule exhausts its candidates and falls back to the deepest module, which is the dropout. The heuristic did not misjudge a trade-off, it was evaluated outside the input regime its constant encodes. This is why the failure clusters on small medical patches rather than on natural-image benchmarks, and it is the practical warning we would want a reader to take away: a target-layer heuristic imported unchanged from a 224\,px pipeline has an unstated precondition that a 96\,px pipeline violates.

Falling back is not itself fatal. Five of the twelve backbones took the fallback path and four of them landed on usable $3\times3$ sites; DenseNet121, DenseNet201 and ConvNeXt-Tiny reach their own best score there. What is fatal is falling back into a network whose terminal modules are pooling and dropout, because the fallback rule is ordered by depth and depth is exactly the direction in which spatial extent disappears.

\textbf{Why the failure is silent rather than loud.} Every operation downstream of the degenerate site is total on degenerate input. Min-max normalization of a constant map is defined and returns zeros. A percentile threshold on an array whose values are all equal is defined and selects the entire frame. Dice between the entire frame and a small mask is defined and, on a fixed sample set, is a constant -- 0.0350 here, appearing identically in all eleven $1\times1$ failures. The pointing rate is defined and returns 0.0000. Not one of these steps has an input it can reject, so the pipeline converts a structural mistake into a number in the same range that a genuinely poor explanation would produce. The reader of such a table sees a network that localizes badly and has no way to see a selector that never localized at all. The remedy is cheap and we recommend it as a default: record the count of identically-zero maps and the spatial size of the resolved site alongside every CAM metric, and treat a $1\times1$ resolution or a non-zero zero-map count as a failed run rather than as a low score. A size check alone is not sufficient -- EfficientNet-B0 fails identically at a $4\times4$ site -- which is why the zero-map count is the load-bearing column and appears in Table~\ref{tab:joint}.

\textbf{Two heuristics, two failure modes, one conclusion.} The canonical rule fails broadly and gracefully: it is best in only 4 of 12 backbones, it never selects the worst site either, and its deficit is a continuum that reaches 0.6333 in Inception-ResNet-v2. The band rule fails narrowly and catastrophically: where its band is populated it is a reasonable pick, and where it is not it can resolve to a site that emits nothing, costing 0.5000 in GoogLeNet. A practitioner cannot tell in advance which regime a given backbone at a given input size falls into, because the diagnostic quantity -- the set of feature-map heights the network actually emits -- is not something either rule reports. That is the argument for reporting a curve instead of a configuration. The sweep is not expensive: 86 sites over twelve backbones on 60 samples, at the per-sample costs in Section~\ref{sec:degenerate}, is a small fraction of the cost of training any one of the models. A curve also makes the shape visible, and the shape carries information a point estimate destroys: nine of twelve backbones peak in the middle, which is what one expects when localization is limited from one side by semantic abstraction and from the other by spatial resolution, and none peaks at the end, which the canonical default implicitly assumes.

The resolution floor is the mechanism behind the two shallow peaks. On a 96\,px patch the mean lesion occupies 1.9\% of the frame. A $2\times2$ map divides that frame into four cells of 25\% each, so its argmax cannot land inside a 1.9\% mask except by coincidence -- and indeed all four backbones whose canonical site is $2\times2$ report exactly 0.0000. ResNet50 and VGG16 recover to 0.3833 and 0.4667 at their shallowest candidates, $32\times32$ and $64\times64$. For lesions this small relative to the field of view, the usual advice to prefer semantic depth over resolution inverts.

\textbf{The protocol axis, and what it does not explain.} On optimizer4, the dominance of optimizer over weight decay by a factor of roughly 2400 in $\eta^2$ is consistent with a learning-rate mismatch rather than with an intrinsic property of SGD: SGD fine-tuning conventionally uses a learning rate one to two orders of magnitude above Adam or AdamW, and this sweep used a single learning rate common to all three. The deficits are exactly where that account predicts them -- 0.146 AUC for EfficientNet-B0 and 0.126 for ResNet50 against Adam, with Adam and AdamW indistinguishable -- so the honest reading is that the sweep measures a protocol interaction and not an optimizer ranking.

That account is now tested rather than asserted. We re-ran the four optimizer4 backbones under SGD at learning rates $\{10^{-3},10^{-2},10^{-1}\}$, five folds each, holding weight decay and every other setting at the values used above. At the shared $10^{-4}$ the deficits reproduce: $-0.1461$, $-0.1268$, $-0.0951$ and $-0.0431$ against Adam for EfficientNet-B0, ResNet50, MobileNetV2 and VGG16. At SGD's own best learning rate, $10^{-2}$, the same four differences become $+0.0382$, $+0.0028$, $+0.0495$ and $-0.0011$. The deficit does not shrink, it disappears, and it reverses on two backbones; no residual gap exceeds 0.0011, which is smaller than the between-fold standard deviation of every model in the sweep (0.0108 to 0.0401). The response is unimodal rather than monotone, so this is not simply an argument for larger steps: at $10^{-1}$, VGG16 returns exactly 0.5000 on all five folds with zero variance, having failed to train at all, and MobileNetV2 and ResNet50 show a three- to four-fold increase in fold-to-fold spread at the same setting. The published grid samples the far-left tail of that curve, and only for SGD, because $10^{-4}$ is an appropriate setting for Adam.

The $\eta^2$ decomposition is unchanged by this and remains correct for the grid it was computed on; what changes is what it licenses. The 2400-fold ratio is evidence that learning rate is the consequential hyperparameter, measured on a design in which optimizer identity was confounded with learning-rate appropriateness, and it is not evidence that the choice among the three optimizers ranks them. We make no claim that SGD is superior: after tuning it is equivalent, three of the four differences fall inside $\pm 0.05$ and the fourth inside $\pm 0.002$, and Adam and AdamW were not themselves swept, so tuning all three could reorder them again. Three learning rates is a bracket, not a search: what these data establish is that $10^{-4}$ is far below the optimum for SGD and $10^{-1}$ is past it. Limitations item 10 accordingly rests on a measurement rather than on an expectation.

The same caution applies to the granularity axis, where an aggregate Friedman ranking and per-model paired DeLong tests disagree: the omnibus reflects a trend consistent across models and folds even where individual models do not individually cross a threshold, and collapsing the two into one headline would overstate the model-level evidence.

Finally, the capacity axis is the one that behaves as the literature already led us to expect, and we treat it as a domain confirmation rather than as a contribution. A 9.7-fold parameter range buying 0.0144 AUC, with six of seven models showing more fold-to-fold variation than that, is the transfer-learning-over-parameterization result restated on a new cohort \cite{raghu_transfusion_2019}. What is worth carrying forward from it is not the correlation, which is not significant at $n=7$, but the Pareto structure: the two cheapest models in the set sit on the front, and the most expensive is dominated on every axis we measured.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{1. Sample size and study design.} Twelve backbones, seven cost-profiled models and four models on the optimizer grid are small samples. Every comparison here is observational, none is a controlled experiment over architectures, and the whole study is hypothesis-generating. No claim in this paper is established beyond the cohort, the tooling and the sample sizes reported with it.

\textbf{2. Both capacity correlations are non-significant, in both directions.} Spearman $\rho=0.50$ at $n=7$ with $p=0.2532$ for parameters against AUC, and $\rho=0.13$ at $n=12$ with $p=0.6860$ for parameters against pointing accuracy. Neither result demonstrates a monotone association, and -- this is the part that is easy to get wrong -- neither is evidence that no association exists. At $n=7$ the study has very little power to detect a moderate correlation, so a null result is uninformative about the underlying effect. The capacity question is left open by these data in both directions, and we state it that way deliberately.

\textbf{3. Three axes on three unequal, non-nested subsets.} localization12, efficiency7 and optimizer4 differ in size and membership; MobileNetV2 appears in optimizer4 and nowhere else, so optimizer4 is not a subset of localization12. Conclusions from one axis do not transfer to the models of another, and the subset column in Table~\ref{tab:joint} exists so that no reader has to reconstruct which models support which claim.

\textbf{4. Family and input resolution are confounded across the twelve.} The five backbones reporting a pointing accuracy of 0.0000 -- MobileNetV3-Small, EfficientNet-B0, ResNet50 and VGG16 at 64\,px, and ViT-Base at 224\,px -- are exactly the five not evaluated at 96\,px. Nothing in the cross-backbone comparison can separate the effect of the architecture family from the effect of the input size, and we do not attempt to. The within-backbone depth sweep is untouched by this confound, because each curve holds the network, the weights, the input size and the 60 samples fixed and varies only the site; that is what keeps the headline claim standing while the cross-backbone reading of Table~\ref{tab:joint} remains ambiguous.

\textbf{5. Localization is not faithfulness.} Overlap with a radiologist mask measures where a map is bright, not whether the network used that region. Our own sanity checks bound this. Under cascading randomization of GoogLeNet at the recovered site, the map's rank correlation with the original stays at 0.8500, 0.8015, 0.7671 and 0.7087 as the classifier and the three deepest stages are randomized in turn, and only collapses -- to 0.0033, then to negative values as low as $-0.2967$ -- once the mid-level stages are destroyed. The map is thus insensitive to the parameters closest to the decision, which is the signature of an activation-driven rather than a decision-driven explanation. The class-flip probe agrees: explaining the non-predicted class yields a mean Spearman of 0.7712 with the original map, so the map is only weakly class-discriminative. A high pointing accuracy in this paper should be read as evidence about the explanation site, not as evidence that the model attends to the lesion. We can now put a direct measurement behind that reading on a subset. On the three backbones this project retrained under nested cross-validation -- ConvNeXt-Tiny, DenseNet121 and DenseNet201, at their canonical sites over the same 60 nodules per fold -- we measure faithfulness with ROAD, which imputes the most-relevant pixels first (MoRF) and the least-relevant first (LeRF) over nine percentiles \cite{rong_road_2022}, and with the prediction gap on important against unimportant features (PGI/PGU), which needs no mask \cite{agarwal_openxai_2022}. A faithful map drives MoRF below LeRF. ConvNeXt-Tiny does so in five folds of five (combined score $+0.0807$, PGI/PGU $=1.96$); DenseNet121 in one of five ($-0.0316$, $1.38$); DenseNet201 in none ($-0.0441$, $0.39$), meaning the pixels its map calls important move the decision \emph{less} than the pixels it calls unimportant. That ordering reproduces the pointing accuracies of these same checkpoints, 0.5000, 0.2500 and 0.0000, from two families of measurement that share no machinery with pointing accuracy or with each other. A caveat travels with this half of the finding: perturbation tests are reported to favor spatial explanations over feature-attribution ones \cite{tempel_choose_2025}, so these numbers rank backbones within one spatial method and must not be read as ranking explanation \emph{families}.

\textbf{6. Stability is not a virtue on its own.} We expected fragile maps to be the signature of a failing explanation, and measured the opposite. Under physical perturbations -- a $\pm3^\circ$ tilt and additive Gaussian noise at $\sigma=0.02$, which at our $[-1000, 400]$\,HU window is 28\,HU -- structural similarity between the map before and after ranks the three backbones exactly backwards from faithfulness \cite{qin_consistency_2025}. DenseNet201, the only backbone whose map is reversed on both faithfulness families, is the \emph{most} stable (SSIM $0.9210$ under tilt, $0.9863$ under noise); ConvNeXt-Tiny, the only faithful one, is the least ($0.8702$, $0.9501$), with DenseNet121 between them on both axes. Cross-patient consistency points the same way: DenseNet201's maps are the most alike across \emph{different} patients sharing a label ($0.5507$ benign, $0.6249$ malignant, against $0.3687$ and $0.4001$ for DenseNet121). The three facts compose into one reading: a map that barely depends on its input is stable under perturbation, similar across patients, and uninformative about the decision, all for the same reason. Stability therefore cannot be reported as an explanation-quality metric on its own -- it is high exactly where the explanation is worth least. We state this as an observation and not as a correlation: three backbones ranked in opposite order is a pattern in a sample of three, and the tilt magnitude and noise level were fixed in advance rather than swept. To our knowledge no prior lung-nodule study reports stability and faithfulness together, which is why the tension is available to us at all.

\textbf{7. The ViT CAM path is broken, not merely weak.} At its published site, \texttt{encoder\_layer\_11.ln\_1}, the map is identically zero on 53 of 60 samples, and the degeneracy worsens monotonically with depth across encoder layers 8, 10 and 11 (27, 50 and 53 zero maps). Layer-CAM on a token-grid architecture with pre-norm blocks is not a validated configuration and our result should not be read as a statement about ViT interpretability. The row is retained in Table~\ref{tab:joint} with that annotation rather than dropped, because a table from which the awkward row has been quietly removed is exactly what a reviewer would ask about. Repairing the path -- attention-aware CAM variants, or an alternative reshaping of the token sequence -- is future work.

\textbf{8. One retrain, two sets of stale numbers, and predictions overwritten in place.} DenseNet121's checkpoint carries a modification time six hours after the timestamp of the metrics originally published against it: the model was retrained after its numbers were written. Re-measuring at the same site under the current checkpoint gives pointing accuracy 0.5833 against a published 0.7167, a gap of 0.1333, with Dice 0.0622 against 0.1030 and size-matched Dice 0.3475 against 0.4703. The current numbers are the ones reported throughout this paper. All other eleven backbones have checkpoints predating their published metrics, and their differences from the published values are attributable to the site correction rather than to a weight change.

The same retrain also reached the per-arm statistics of Section~\ref{sec:optimizer}, and how it did so is worth stating precisely, because the failure mode is more instructive than the discrepancy. Of the 120 stored prediction files, exactly five differ between the pre-revision state and the current one: the five DenseNet121 binary folds. The ordinal, three-class and four-class arms are byte-identical across all thirty files each, which is why the ordinal-native metrics above are unchanged to four decimal places while the Friedman statistic is not. An earlier internal figure of $\chi^2=43.96$ lies between a pre-revision value of 41.48 and the current 44.52, matching neither, and the mean-rank vector printed with it implies a DenseNet121 rank block lying between the two as well. Those figures were therefore computed from a DenseNet121 prediction state intermediate between those two, which the retrain overwrote in place and which no longer exists on disk. No statistic was ever miscomputed: each was correct for the predictions it was given, and those predictions went stale without any artifact recording that they had. The controllable cause is that predictions were written to a single unversioned path, so a retrain silently replaced the evidence for numbers already in a draft. Subsequent runs in this project write predictions under a run-scoped directory and stamp every result row with the run identifier, commit hash and checkpoint modification time, which makes exactly this staleness detectable rather than invisible.

\textbf{9. Single dataset, no external validation.} Everything here is LIDC-IDRI, one fold assignment, one preprocessing pipeline. Neither the localization result nor the efficiency result has been reproduced on an independent cohort, and the degenerate-site rate in particular is a joint property of these networks, these input sizes and this tooling.

\textbf{10. Identical hyperparameters across architectures.} All twelve backbones share one training recipe. That is what makes the comparison controlled, and it is also what makes it unfair to any family the recipe suits badly -- the SGD deficit in Section~\ref{sec:optimizer} is a visible instance of the same problem on the protocol axis. A per-architecture tuning budget could reorder the backbones, and evidence that it does exists in the benchmarking literature already cited. On the optimizer axis this limitation is no longer a conjecture: the learning-rate sweep in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} shows a shared setting suppressing one optimizer by up to 0.146 AUC and the entire gap closing once that setting is released. We have measured the cost of a shared recipe on one hyperparameter of one axis; the same exposure on the remaining hyperparameters is untested.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Across twelve backbones on LIDC-IDRI, the variable that governs class-activation-map localization is not the architecture family and not the parameter count. It is the layer at which the map is taken. Over 86 candidate explanation sites, pointing accuracy moves by up to 0.7333 within a single frozen network, the canonical last-spatial-layer default is optimal in only 4 of 12 backbones, and in none of the twelve is the deepest candidate the best one. Both automatic selectors we tested fail, in different ways: the canonical rule fails broadly and gracefully, and the band rule -- whose $[7,10]$ constant silently encodes a 224\,px input -- can resolve to a site with no spatial extent, which is what produced one of our own published rows of exactly 0.0000 pointing accuracy. That failure is silent by construction, because a constant map still yields a finite Dice of 0.0350; 17 of the 86 sites emit an identically-zero map on at least one sample and 12 do so on every sample, in all twelve backbones. Capacity and protocol are reported alongside and behave as domain expectation predicts: a 9.7-fold parameter range spans 0.0144 AUC, less than the fold-to-fold variation of six of seven models, while on a separate four-backbone grid optimizer choice accounts for roughly 2400 times more AUC variance than weight decay, an artifact of one shared learning rate that a follow-up sweep resolves rather than an intrinsic optimizer ranking. The operational recommendation is narrow and cheap: report the resolved site, its spatial size and its count of identically-zero maps with every CAM number, and report a depth curve rather than a single configuration; without that audit, a cross-architecture explainability table may be describing the tooling rather than the networks. A companion paper addresses radiomics-CNN fusion and per-arm explainability on the same cohort and the same fold assignment.
